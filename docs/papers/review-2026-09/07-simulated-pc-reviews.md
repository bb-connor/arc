## P1: "Receiver-Owned Bilateral Admission for Cross-Organization Agent Tool Calls" (docs/papers/programmable-sovereignty, paper-usenix.tex build, 10 body pages; reviewed with README.md, CLAIM_LEDGER.md, supplementary/README.md, and the pinned bench/results files as the artifact appendix)

Venue: USENIX Security 2027. Persona: Systems-security PC member; expert in authorization systems and capability operating systems; skeptical of novelty claims made over existing signed-attestation and verifier-owned-policy work (in-toto layouts, TUF, Macaroons, SCITT, dual control).. Score 2 / 5, confidence 4 / 5.

### Summary

The paper describes a pre-dispatch admission check that the receiving kernel of the Chio agent runtime performs before invoking a tool on behalf of a request that originated in another organization. The receiver takes no trust material from the request: it resolves the treaty record (participants, keys, permitted action classes, validity window, revocation epoch, ladder-manifest digests), the continuation, lineage, prior receipts, leases, and governance records from its own stores by identifier, and then verifies a DSSE envelope of predicate type chio.bilateral-cosign-invocation.v1 that must carry exactly two signatures from distinct configured keys over one RFC 8785 canonical statement binding the request, outcome, treaty, continuation, lineage, lease, governance references, and both receipt digests. Continuations are reserved during admission, consumed once on the admitted path, and released on denial. Accepted material is carried as a validated Rust type (VerifiedFederationTreatyMaterial) into receipt construction so that the signed receipt cannot be assembled from a different request or participant snapshot. Separately, a bounded receipt-predicate library (nine atom forms, boolean connectives, constitutions as predicate lists) is modeled in Lean 4, where a finite-domain refinement checker refinesOn(K',K,D) is proved equivalent to "for every r in D, admits(K',r) implies admits(K,r)"; two independently written Rust evaluators of that library are differentially tested on every atom and 1,024 generated cases per compound property. The evaluation reports that 20 hand-authored negative cases all deny with the expected code; that the full pre-dispatch denial path, including a signed SQLite denial receipt, costs 4.1 ms p50 / 9.8 ms p99 over 30 samples with the tool invocation counter at zero; that a three-vendor single-host workflow producing and verifying a 51.8 KB buyer package costs 2.37 s p50 over 20 runs; and component microbenchmarks (bilateral DSSE verify 101 us, receipt sign 135 us, SQLite append 3.2 ms). The authors are explicit that two configured keys do not establish two independent organizations (PS-A-01), that the Lean model does not verify the Rust runtime, and that the workflow number is not hook latency.

### Strengths

- The framing in Sections 1 and 7 is correct and worth restating for the agent-systems audience: a signature authenticates bytes, it does not authorize, and the verifier must resolve the referenced context (treaty, keys, revocation, continuation) from state it owns rather than from the request. The concrete rule that request metadata may carry identifiers but never trust roots, peer directories, keys, or dynamic trust bundles (Section 3.2 step 3, Section 5.1) is the right fail-closed discipline and is enforced in code, not policy.
- The paper is unusually disciplined about the boundary of its claims. PS-A-01 (one actor holding both keys passes the cryptographic profile), the statement that the differential suite compares Rust with Rust and is not a refinement proof, the statement that the 2.37 s workflow figure is not receiver-hook latency, and the CLAIM_LEDGER.md with a 'Not established' section are all the kind of hygiene reviewers ask for and rarely get.
- The engineering is real and specific: named stable failure codes, a continuation reserve/consume/release lifecycle around the admission window, a typed carrier that couples the accepted admission result to receipt construction (Section 5.3), a dispatch-capable benchmark that observes a tool invocation counter rather than inferring non-dispatch, and a content-addressed artifact manifest that pins symbols rather than line numbers.
- The strict DSSE profile is specified precisely enough to implement: exactly two signatures, distinct key identifiers, both over the same PAE bytes, one subject, rejection of duplicate keyids and of two keyids resolving to one public key, rejection of noncanonical JSON and unknown statement or predicate types (Section 5.2).
- The paper is within the page budget (10 of 13 pages), the writing is plain, and the tables of assumptions (Table 3) and surfaces (Table 1) make it easy to see what was and was not evaluated.

### Weaknesses

- W1 (Sections 1, 2, 8; novelty). The central mechanism, a verifier-owned policy naming expected signers, a threshold of two signatures over one canonical statement, freshness via expiry and a monotone revocation epoch, and binding of the statement to the specific request and nonce, is the standard verifier discipline of in-toto layouts (expected steps, functionaries, per-step key thresholds, verified against a layout the verifier holds), TUF (verifier-owned root, delegation thresholds, expiry, snapshot freshness), Macaroons and Biscuit (context-bound caveats checked by the target), SCITT (receiver-side registration policy), and, most directly, decades of dual-control / M-of-N authorization in HSMs and banking. Section 8 cites in-toto and SLSA only as 'build provenance' and dismisses them on that basis; that mischaracterizes in-toto, whose layout and verification half is precisely a receiver-owned expected-signer policy. TUF, Macaroons, Biscuit, SCITT, UCAN/ZCAP, SPIFFE trust bundles, OAuth DPoP/token binding, and M-of-N dual control are not cited at all. The paper never states what a receiver following an in-toto layout with two functionaries, threshold 2, an audience field, and a nonce would fail to catch that bilateral admission catches. Without that argument the contribution is 'we applied the known verifier discipline to agent tool calls', which is a fine engineering note but not a USENIX Security research contribution.
- W2 (Section 2, security objective). The stated objective, A_R(q)=allow implies coreAllows(q) and receiverEvidenceAccepts(q), is satisfied by a kernel that denies everything, and receiverEvidenceAccepts is defined only by the English gloss 'fresh, mutually bound, strictly verified, and accepted by receiver policy'. There is no unforgeability-style statement (e.g. allow implies that the two configured keys for the resolved treaty signed exactly the tuple the receiver reconstructed for this request and this continuation, and that tuple admits no other request), no completeness statement for an honest sender, and no argument, even a game-based sketch, that the gate conjunction of Section 3.2 excludes every substitution listed in the adversary paragraph. The only formal result in the paper (Section 4) is about a different object that the paper itself says 'is not an alternate authorization path' and 'does not grant access'. The formal component therefore does not touch the security claim.
- W3 (Section 4; the Lean result is definitional and its role is unmotivated). refinesOn is defined as a fold of (admits(K',r) implies admits(K,r)) over the list D, and finite_refinement_exact states that this boolean equals the universally quantified Prop over the same list. That is List.all versus forall, an unfolding, not a theorem about the protocol. The companion manuscript by the same group (P2, Section 4) describes its parallel accept-set theorem candidly as 'definitional' and 'one Lean tactic line'; P1 should be equally candid instead of listing this as one of four headline contributions. More importantly, P1 never explains who proposes a candidate constitution K', when refinesOn is invoked in deployment, or how its verdict affects admission. As written, Section 4 and RQ2 evaluate a toy nine-atom language that sits beside the real verifier and has no stated operational use.
- W4 (Section 6.2, RQ1; the adversarial evaluation is a hand-written unit-test suite). The 20 negative cases were authored by the people who wrote the checks, cover one instance per named failure, and are the entire evidence for the abstract's claim that the implementation 'rejects missing, stale, replayed, and mismatched evidence'. There is no mutation campaign over the tuple fields of the envelope, no property-based or fuzz testing of verify_chio_bilateral_invocation, no generated adversarial envelopes, and no attempt to find a case that passes. Section 9 concedes that some of the 20 stop in the envelope verifier 'before a dispatch-capable runtime surface exists', so the RQ1 title 'Do attacks deny before dispatch?' is answered by the invocation counter for only a subset, and the paper does not say which subset. For a paper whose thesis is that substitution is 'the difficult part', this is thin.
- W5 (Section 6.1, CLAIM_LEDGER.md, bench/results; the measurement record is internally inconsistent). (a) CLAIM_LEDGER.md reports the headline pre-dispatch denial as 11.124 ms p50 / 20.530 ms p99 and the workflow as 2.488 s / 3.291 s; the paper's inline macros and bilateral-admission.json report 4.115 ms / 9.790 ms and 2.370 s / 3.379 s, a 2.7x difference on the headline number. (b) Section 6.1 says the experiments ran on Linux/aarch64 with eight Neoverse-N1 cores and 49.2 GB RAM; the environment file that the artifact manifest pins by SHA-256 (bilateral-admission-environment.txt) records host aarch64-apple-darwin, Darwin MacBookPro 25.4.0, ARM64_T6000, i.e. an Apple M1 Pro/Max laptop. (c) supplementary/source-commit.txt (dfcd5116...) differs from the commit recorded in every benchmark result file (3dfd241e...). Any one of these might be a stale-file slip; together they undermine the artifact discipline the paper advertises, and one of the two machine descriptions is simply false.
- W6 (Abstract, Sections 6.3, 10; p99 over n=20 and n=30 is not a p99). With 20 workflow samples the reported p99 (3378.651 ms) is exactly the maximum sample (row 19 of bilateral-admission-raw.csv), and with 30 component samples it is at best an interpolation between the two largest. Section 6.6 concedes the sample counts 'are too small to support operational tail-latency targets', yet p99 figures are headlined in the abstract and conclusion. Report min/median/max or raise n by two orders of magnitude.
- W7 (Sections 3, 5; the protocol is never actually specified). There is no message sequence: who produces the bilateral predicate, in what order, over which not-yet-known values, and when each of the two signatures is applied relative to the receiver's admission decision. Figure 1 shows Participant A and Participant B both signing and a separate receiver verifying, but Section 5.3 says the constructor checks 'the request's origin and local participant', implying the receiver is one of the two participants. If the receiver is one signer, it is verifying its own signature, and the paper must explain what that establishes and how the receiver signed a predicate containing its own local receipt digest before admitting. If the two signers are the vendor and a third kernel, say so and define the third party's role. Relatedly, the tuple binds an 'outcome digest', but at pre-dispatch time the tool has not run; which outcome is meant is never defined.
- W8 (Section 2; the hardest problem is assumed away). Step 2 of the admission sequence says 'trusted runtime state classifies the request' as local or federated, and Section 2 assumes 'the receiver classifies federated origin correctly'. A federated request misclassified as local bypasses every check in the paper. How classification is established (transport identity, mTLS peer, capability provenance) is not described, and it is not in Table 3's assumption list.
- W9 (Sections 3.1, 5.1; continuation, concurrency, and oracle semantics are underspecified). Denial 'releases' the continuation rather than consuming it, so an adversary can re-present the same continuation indefinitely while varying other fields and reading the stable named failure code, which gives a per-gate oracle over the tuple. The paper does not discuss what a sender learns from the roughly twenty distinct diagnostic codes. Reservation atomicity under two concurrent requests naming the same continuation, and behavior on crash between consume and dispatch, are excluded (Section 9) rather than designed. Continuation single-use is enforced only at one receiver; replay of the same continuation to a second receiver in the same treaty is not addressed. Cross-organization clock skew for validity intervals and the latency of learning a peer's revocation are not discussed.
- W10 (Sections 3.1, 5.2, Table 3; canonicalization and domain separation). Receipts are 'signed over canonical JSON' with the kernel key, and the same kernel key signs DSSE PAE bytes. DSSE PAE provides a domain tag for the envelope, but the paper never states that the receipt-body signature is domain-separated from the envelope signature or from any other object that key signs; cross-protocol confusion under one Ed25519 key should be ruled out explicitly. The paper also never says whether the verifier rejects noncanonical input or re-canonicalizes it (P2 says rejects; P1 is silent), and the receipt's free-form 'evidence' and 'metadata' fields have no stated size or shape bound in the signed path.
- W11 (Section 6; no baseline). There is no comparison, quantitative or argued, against the obvious alternatives: a mutual-TLS channel with an audience-restricted, nonce-bound token; an in-toto layout verification with two functionaries; or a single-signer receipt plus receiver policy. The reader cannot tell what the second signature buys given PS-A-01, nor what the treaty machinery buys over a signed allowlist.
- W12 (Section 3.1; undefined vocabulary). The ladder order 'observation, guarded, receipt-backed, partition-contingency, quorum-required' is asserted without definitions; 'partition-contingency' and 'quorum-required' are never explained and the latter is odd in a strictly bilateral construction. 'Programmable sovereignty' appears in Section 7 without introduction (a residue of an earlier title). Table 1 is largely a symbol index that only a reader with the repository can use.
- W13 (double-blind hygiene). The system name Chio and symbol names such as ChioRuntimeAdmissionHook and chio.bilateral-cosign-invocation.v1 identify a public open-source project; combined with the companion manuscript's affiliation line this effectively deanonymizes the submission. The chairs should look at this.

### Detailed comments

- Abstract and Section 10: 'proves that its finite-domain refinement checker accepts exactly when the candidate predicate admits no receipt in the supplied domain that the current predicate rejects' is a correct description of a definitional identity; the sentence should not carry the weight of a formal contribution. Say what P2 says about its parallel theorem: it is a drift guard, not a security theorem.
- Section 1, motivating example: the buyer receives a signed refund receipt from a vendor and must reject it if it belongs to another treaty. That is post-hoc verification by the buyer of a receipt. The protocol in Sections 3 and 5 is pre-dispatch verification by the receiving kernel of the tool call. These are different parties at different times, and the paper alternates between them (pre-dispatch hook versus buyer package review). Fix the roles up front: buyer, vendor A, vendor B, receiving kernel, offline verifier.
- Section 2, 'Adversary': the list is good, but the adversary 'may control a remote vendor kernel' without any statement of what an honest sender must do. Add an honest-participant obligation list so completeness can be stated; right now the protocol has no completeness property and could be vacuously secure.
- Section 2, security objective: replace or supplement with a statement of the form 'if A_R(q)=allow then there exist signatures by both keys configured for treaty T, resolved from receiver state, over the exact canonical tuple the receiver reconstructed from q, and that tuple's continuation was unconsumed at the time of the check and is consumed afterward'. That is provable about the real gate conjunction, in Lean or Tamarin, and it is what the threat model needs.
- Section 3.2, step 6 lists 'policy agreement' among the DSSE verifier checks; Section 5.2 lists 'policy results' among the receiver-state comparisons. Define what policy agreement means: agreement between the two signers' recorded verdicts, or agreement with the receiver's local verdict? PS-TH-19 'disagreement' and PS-TH-20 'unanimous deny' suggest both signers embed a verdict in the predicate, but the tuple description in Section 3.1 does not list a verdict field.
- Section 3.2, 'Failure evidence': 'Envelope-only cases stop earlier, before entering an API that can dispatch a tool' means non-dispatch was not observed for those cases. State explicitly, per case ID, which of PS-TH-01..20 ran through the dispatch-capable hook with the counter.
- Section 4: the receipt view includes modeRank and liveContinuations, which are things the hook has already checked. If the bounded library re-evaluates facts the hook has already enforced, its only role is offline policy analysis; say that and move the section to an appendix, or drop it.
- Section 5.1: the hook 'reserves the continuation while admission is in progress'. Say whether the reservation is a store-level atomic compare-and-set, what the store's durability guarantee is for the consume before dispatch, and what happens if the kernel crashes between consume and the tool call (the continuation is burned and the action never happened) or between the tool call and the receipt append (the action happened and the record is lost).
- Section 5.3: the VerifiedFederationTreatyMaterial argument is the strongest structural claim in the paper (no receipt can be built without a value of this type, and the type can only be built from an accepted admission). State it as an invariant ('the tool dispatch path for a federated request requires a value of type X, and X has a single constructor that requires ...') so that the zero-invocation counter becomes corroboration of a type-level argument rather than the sole evidence.
- Section 6.3: the component numbers already explain the denial path: 3.19 ms of the 4.12 ms p50 is the SQLite append, and DSSE verification is 0.10 ms. Say plainly that cryptography is negligible and the cost is the durable denial receipt; that is a useful systems finding and it is buried.
- Section 6.3: 'cross-boundary admission allow 14.4 us' is never explained; it is much smaller than the 101 us bilateral verify it supposedly includes. Define what is inside this timed body.
- Section 6.4: the 50 generic capability fixtures are unrelated to this paper's claims (the paper says so). Remove them; they only invite the inflation you are disclaiming.
- Section 6.1 versus the pinned environment file: reconcile Linux/Neoverse-N1/49.2 GB with Darwin/MacBookPro/T6000, and reconcile CLAIM_LEDGER.md with the inline macros and the JSON. If the ledger reflects an earlier run, regenerate it from the same run as the paper; if the paper reflects a laptop run, say so and drop the server description.
- Table 3: add 'federated-origin classification is correct' and 'participant clocks agree within the validity-interval tolerance' as assumptions; both are load-bearing and both are currently only mentioned in passing or not at all.
- Section 8: rewrite the 'Authenticated evidence' paragraph to engage in-toto layouts (expected signers and thresholds), TUF, SCITT registration policy, Macaroons/Biscuit context-bound caveats, and M-of-N dual control, and say specifically what substitution each of them would miss. Also engage the agent-side authorization literature beyond IsolateGPT and SAGA (MCP authorization, Progent-style policy enforcement, AgentDojo-style adversarial evaluation) so that the evaluation methodology can be compared.
- Companion manuscript (P2, 'Bilateral Receipt Admission: Cross-Organizational Action Provenance with Treaty-Bound DSSE'): it describes the same predicate type, the same strict verifier, the same three-vendor closure, and a different Lean artifact (BilateralAccept.lean, three abstract gates), with different numbers (72 us allow path on an M1 Max; five rejection codes rather than a twenty-case matrix) and an affiliation line naming the project. The PC should check for overlapping submission, and if both are in flight the papers should cite each other's exact division of labor rather than describing the other as 'anonymized for review, under separate submission'. P2's Section 3 also documents an adaptive gate-ordering oracle that P1 does not mention despite exposing more distinct failure codes.
- Presentation: the paper has three pages of budget left. Spend them on a protocol section with a message sequence chart, a worked envelope (P2 Section 3 has one), a per-case table of the 20 negative cases with their enforcement phase and whether the counter was observed, and definitions of the ladder modes.

### Questions for the authors

- Who are the two signers relative to the receiving kernel? If the receiver is one of them, what does verifying its own signature establish, and at what point relative to the admission decision was that signature produced over a predicate that includes the receiver's own local receipt digest?
- How does a pre-dispatch check bind an 'outcome digest' for a tool that has not yet run? Which outcome is meant, and who produced it?
- Please reconcile: CLAIM_LEDGER.md (11.124 ms / 20.530 ms; 2.488 s / 3.291 s) versus the paper (4.115 ms / 9.790 ms; 2.370 s / 3.379 s); Section 6.1's Linux/Neoverse-N1/49.2 GB machine versus the pinned environment file's Darwin/MacBookPro/T6000; and source-commit.txt (dfcd5116) versus the benchmark commit (3dfd241e). Which numbers and which machine are the ones the paper stands on?
- How is federated origin classified, and what is the consequence if a federated request is classified as local? Why is this not in the threat model's assumption table?
- Of the 20 negative cases, exactly which ones ran through the dispatch-capable hook with the invocation counter observed, and which stopped in the envelope verifier?
- Why does a denial release the continuation instead of consuming it, and what does a sender learn from the roughly twenty distinct stable denial codes across repeated attempts with the same continuation?
- What is the operational role of refinesOn? Who proposes a candidate constitution K', when is the check run, who chooses D, and what happens to admission if the check fails?
- Is the Ed25519 receipt-body signature domain-separated from the DSSE PAE signature and from any other object the same kernel key signs? Does the verifier reject noncanonical JSON or re-canonicalize?
- What substitution or replay in your adversary list would a receiver enforcing an in-toto layout with two functionaries and threshold 2, plus an audience field and a nonce, fail to reject? Same question for a TUF-style delegation with threshold 2.
- Under two concurrent requests naming the same continuation, is reservation an atomic compare-and-set in every supported store? What is the state after a crash between consume and dispatch, and between dispatch and receipt append?

### The one change that would most move the score

Replace Section 4 with a precise protocol specification (roles, message sequence, what each signature covers and when) and a non-vacuous security statement about the real gate conjunction (allow implies both treaty-configured keys signed exactly the tuple the receiver reconstructed for this request and unconsumed continuation, and no other request can reuse it), supported by a machine-checked model of the actual verifier under the substitution adversary (Tamarin/ProVerif, or Lean over the real tuple) and by a field-by-field mutation and fuzz campaign over the envelope rather than 20 hand-written cases; and ship a single consistent measurement record (one machine description, one commit, one set of numbers) with sample sizes that justify the percentiles reported. That change would move this from weak reject to accept territory; nothing else in the paper moves the score as much.


## P1: Receiver-Owned Bilateral Admission for Cross-Organization Agent Tool Calls (docs/papers/programmable-sovereignty; reviewed from paper.tex, paper-usenix.tex, sections/01-10, CLAIM_LEDGER.md, README.md, supplementary/README.md, the artifact manifest and the bench result files the paper source inputs)

Venue: IEEE S&P. Persona: Applied-cryptography reviewer: security argument, threat-model completeness, the two-keys-one-operator gap, and whether the formal claims are real. Score 2 / 5, confidence 4 / 5.

### Summary

The paper argues that a receiving organization cannot authorize a cross-organization agent tool call from a vendor signature alone, and proposes "receiver-owned bilateral admission": before dispatch, the receiving kernel resolves the treaty record, authorized keys, continuation, lineage, and prior receipts from its own stores (never from request metadata), verifies a DSSE envelope carrying exactly two signatures from two distinct configured keys over one canonical (RFC 8785) statement of predicate type chio.bilateral-cosign-invocation.v1, checks that every referenced digest (request, outcome, treaty scope, ladder intersection, continuation, lineage, both receipts, lease, governance) describes the same invocation, consumes the continuation once, and returns a named denial or permits dispatch. Accepted evidence is carried into receipt construction as a validated type. The paper is implemented in the Chio Rust runtime. Formal support consists of a Lean 4 model of a small bounded receipt-predicate language with a finite-domain refinement checker and two theorems about that checker, plus Rust-vs-Rust differential testing (every atom, 1,024 cases for each of three properties). The evaluation runs 20 hand-written negative cases, a dispatch-capable denial benchmark with a zero-invocation counter, a three-vendor SQLite loopback workflow producing a buyer package, and component microbenchmarks. The authors explicitly disclaim that two keys imply two operators (PS-A-01), that Lean verifies the runtime, or that the single-host numbers generalize.

### Strengths

- The core design principle is correct and clearly articulated: the receiver resolves trust roots, peer keys, revocation state, and continuations from its own stores, and anything in the request that tries to carry a trust root denies (Sec 3, Sec 5.1). This closes the self-appointed-trust-domain failure that generic signed-attestation pipelines have.
- Carrying the accepted admission result into receipt construction as a validated type (VerifiedFederationTreatyMaterial, Sec 5.3) and rechecking request, participants, tool, and action hash at receipt time is sound engineering that prevents an accepted report from being spliced onto a different request.
- The continuation reserve / consume / release lifecycle (Sec 3.1, Sec 5.1) is the right shape for one-shot admission, and replay of a consumed continuation is in the negative corpus.
- Conservative primitives (Ed25519, SHA-256, DSSE PAE, RFC 8785) and a strict envelope profile (exactly two signatures, distinct key ids, rejection of reuse of one public key, rejection of non-canonical JSON, one subject).
- Unusually honest scoping. The paper, CLAIM_LEDGER, and supplementary README repeatedly and consistently say what is not established: two keys do not prove two organizations, Lean does not verify Rust, the finite domain says nothing outside itself, and single-host numbers are not SLOs.
- Artifact discipline is above the norm: a content-addressed manifest naming symbols rather than line numbers, theorem declarations with their axiom lists (propext, Quot.sound), stable negative-case identifiers with expected codes and enforcement phase, and a dispatch counter asserted to remain zero.
- The measured hot path is cheap: strict bilateral DSSE verification is on the order of 100 to 450 microseconds and the end-to-end denial path is dominated by the SQLite denial receipt, so cost is not an argument against deploying the check.

### Weaknesses

- W1 (Sec 2, Sec 3). There is no protocol. The paper describes the receiver's local control flow (Sec 3.2) but never specifies the message flow: who constructs the bilateral statement, in what order the two keys sign, what is exchanged between kernels, and how the receiving kernel's own signature can be over a statement the receiving kernel then verifies before dispatch. Fig 1 shows two participants signing and a receiver verifying, but Sec 5.3 makes the receiver one of the participants. The statement also binds an outcome digest and both receipt digests (Sec 3.1) even though admission is pre-dispatch; the paper never says what outcome exists before the tool runs. A reader cannot reconstruct the verifier from the text.
- W2 (Sec 2, Security objective). The stated security objective, A_R(q)=allow implies coreAllows(q) and receiverEvidenceAccepts(q), is not a security property. Neither predicate is defined; receiverEvidenceAccepts is described in prose as 'fresh, mutually bound, strictly verified, and accepted by receiver policy', which is a restatement of the implementation's own checks. Any receiver-side policy check satisfies this trivially. The paper's own framing (Sec 1: 'the difficult part is preventing substitution') calls for a binding property: if the receiver accepts envelope E for request q under treaty T, then both configured keys produced signatures over PAE bytes whose fields equal the receiver's resolved state for (q, T), and no adversary without both keys can produce such an E for a (q', T') the receiver did not resolve. That property is never stated, never argued from EUF-CMA of Ed25519, collision resistance of SHA-256, and injectivity of JCS, and never mechanized.
- W3 (Sec 4, Sec 5.4, Abstract). The formal claims are real but do not touch the security-critical path. finite_refinement_exact says a list-fold implementation of a bounded universal quantifier equals the quantifier; it is a definitional unfolding. The bounded predicate library it models 'is not an alternate authorization path' and 'does not grant access on behalf of the admission hook' (Sec 5.4). So the only mechanized component is one that, by the paper's own statement, plays no role in the admission decision. Billing this in the abstract and contribution list as the paper's executable model invites a reader to credit formal evidence to a protocol that has none. The Rust-vs-Rust differential test (1,024 cases per property) likewise exercises this library, not verify_chio_bilateral_invocation or the hook.
- W4 (Sec 2, Sec 7, PS-A-01). The two-keys-one-operator gap is acknowledged but its consequence for the paper's thesis is not confronted. The threat model lets the adversary control the remote vendor kernel and both keys the receiver has authorized. Under that adversary the second signature adds nothing to authorization: the receiver's own policy decides, and the vendor signature is evidence for third parties (the buyer package), not an admission input. The paper therefore needs to say what the bilateral signature buys over a unilateral receiver check, and against which adversary. As written, 'bilateral admission' is a unilateral admission decision plus a co-signed audit record, and the security argument for the record (offline verify_package) is not given either.
- W5 (Sec 2, Trusted components; Sec 3.2 step 2). The whole mechanism is gated on 'trusted runtime state classifies the request' as federated. If a cross-organization request can be presented as local, every treaty check is bypassed. Classification is asserted to be trusted and never described; mTLS setup and transport are explicitly excluded from the evaluation (Sec 9). The most consequential gate in the design is outside both the argument and the experiments.
- W6 (Artifact appendix, Sec 6.1, CLAIM_LEDGER). The submission artifact pins mutually inconsistent evidence. The manifest-pinned paper-usenix.pdf (sha256 1321...4249, hash verified) and the manifest-pinned CLAIM_LEDGER report a pre-dispatch denial of 11.124 ms p50 / 20.530 ms p99 and a buyer workflow of 2.488 s / 3.291 s, and Sec 6.1 states the machine is Linux/aarch64 with eight Neoverse-N1 cores and 49.2 GB. The manifest-pinned bench/results files that paper.tex \input{}s (bilateral-admission-inline.tex sha256 2777...290c, bilateral-admission.json, the CSVs; all hashes verified) report 4.115 ms / 9.790 ms and 2.370 s / 3.379 s, and every component row differs (strict bilateral DSSE verify 436.660 us in the PDF versus 101.079 us pinned; SQLite append 7841 us versus 3186 us; offline package verify 50363 us versus 15164 us; negative matrix 15.7 s versus 10.7 s). The pinned environment file records aarch64-apple-darwin on a MacBookPro (Apple M1 family), not Linux Neoverse-N1, and a benchmark commit (3dfd241e) that is not the artifact source commit (dfcd5116). Rebuilding the paper from its own pinned inputs produces a different paper with a wrong Method section. The content-addressing verifies bytes; it does not verify that the bytes agree.
- W7 (Sec 6.2). The adversarial evaluation is 20 hand-written cases. There is no fuzzing of the DSSE envelope parser or canonicalizer, no mutation-based test that perturbs any single field of an accepted evidence graph and asserts denial, and no property-based test of the binding across the full statement. For a paper whose thesis is substitution resistance, the natural experiment (accept a valid graph, then for each field and each artifact substitute a value from a second valid graph and confirm denial with the expected code) is absent.
- W8 (Sec 3.1, Sec 5.2). The statement schema is never shown. Fields are listed by name (treaty reference, admission report, lineage bundle, lease and governance references, consistency model) but not typed, and no worked envelope is given. Without the schema the reader cannot assess field-level domain separation, whether the outcome digest is well-defined pre-dispatch, or which values are compared against which store entries.
- W9 (Sec 3.1, Sec 5.1). Replay defense depends on the continuation being consumed exactly once, yet durability 'depends on the configured store' (CLAIM_LEDGER) and crash recovery during consumption is excluded (Sec 9). No argument is given for atomicity between consume and dispatch or between dispatch and receipt write. Clocks bound continuation and lease validity but a clock adversary is not in the model.
- W10 (Sec 8). Novelty over existing constructions is not established. in-toto layouts and TUF already give verifier-pinned root keys with threshold multi-party signatures over typed statements; DSSE envelopes already support multiple signatures with verifier-side thresholds. Receiver-owned bilateral admission reads as a DSSE 2-of-2 policy with a new predicate type and store lookups. SCITT and COSE receipts, Macaroons and Biscuit (cross-organization attenuation), RFC 8693 token exchange, and RATS are not discussed. The paper should also justify two independent signatures over a 2-of-2 multisignature or aggregate signature, which would make 'same bytes' structural.
- W11 (Sec 3.2 step 7, Sec 5.2). Named failure codes are returned to the sender and written to signed denial receipts. The paper does not consider the error oracle this creates: an adversary that controls the remote kernel can learn, one binding at a time, which treaty id, continuation, or lease the receiver expects. The companion manuscript on the same system discusses exactly this oracle; this paper is silent.
- W12 (Sec 6.3). Only the denial path is measured through the real hook with dispatch capability. The allow path through the hook plus tool dispatch plus receipt binding, which is the path a deployment actually pays for, is not measured end-to-end; 'cross-boundary admission allow' at 14 to 20 us is a library call. Sample sizes of 20 and 30 make the reported p99 essentially the maximum, which the authors admit.

### Detailed comments

- Abstract and Sec 1. The abstract leads with two things the paper does not deliver in the strength the wording suggests: 'a protocol' (no message flow is given) and 'a Lean 4 model ... proves' (the proved statement concerns a library that does not decide admission). The four contributions are honestly worded individually, but the ordering and emphasis let a reader infer that the bilateral construction has been modeled. Recommend re-titling contribution three as 'a mechanized specification of an auxiliary receipt-predicate library' or removing it from the contribution list.
- Sec 2, Adversary. The adversary is allowed to control the remote vendor kernel and both authorized keys. Under this adversary, please state explicitly which of the 20 negative cases still deny and which rely on the vendor key being honest. My reading: cases 08 to 12 (envelope syntax) and 15 (continuation replay) hold against a two-key adversary; the binding cases hold only in the sense that the adversary cannot make the receiver's stores say something else, which is a property of the store, not of the signature. This taxonomy would clarify what the cryptography contributes.
- Sec 2, Security objective. Please define coreAllows and receiverEvidenceAccepts as predicates over concrete inputs (request, resolved store state, envelope). Then state the binding property as a lemma: acceptance implies existence of two signatures under the two resolved public keys over PAE(payloadType, canonicalBytes(S)) where S.fields equal the resolved values, and argue it from EUF-CMA plus collision resistance plus JCS injectivity on the permitted value domain. This is a one-page argument and it is the paper's actual security content.
- Sec 3.1, Bilateral predicate. 'Both signatures must cover the same predicate bytes' is guaranteed only if both signatures are checked against the same PAE bytes from one envelope. Please state that the verifier derives the message once from the envelope and checks both signatures over it, and that key ids are matched to configured public keys before signature verification (so a key id in the envelope cannot select an unexpected key). Also state how a signature's key id is bound to the participant slot (local versus remote), since a swap of the two signature slices should be harmless but a swap of participant identity in the statement should not.
- Sec 3.1, Treaty scope and ladder. If the latter, a stale or misconfigured intersection record weakens the 'stricter participant mode' rule without any signature failing. Sec 3.2 step 4 suggests it is looked up.

If lookups precede signature checks, an unauthenticated request can drive store reads (a cheap DoS on SQLite); if signature checks precede lookups, the keys must be resolvable from identifiers alone. - Sec 4. The theorem is stated correctly and honestly limited to the supplied domain D. Please add its proof size (it should be a few lines) so that reviewers calibrate its weight. If the authors want formal evidence for the paper's thesis, the right target is a Lean (or Verus, given the Rust codebase) statement of the strict envelope profile: exactly two signatures, distinct key ids mapping to distinct configured keys, both over one PAE, and the field-equality checks against a resolved-state record.

- Sec 7, Two keys and two operators. Consider renaming the construction so that the cryptographic guarantee (two configured keys over identical bytes) and the intended organizational guarantee are not conflated.

## P1: Receiver-Owned Bilateral Admission for Cross-Organization Agent Tool Calls (docs/papers/programmable-sovereignty; USENIX Security 2027 target, reviewed here against SOSP/OSDI standards)

Venue: SOSP/OSDI. Persona: Systems reviewer: evaluation methodology, what is actually measured, reproducibility, and deployability. Score 2 / 5, confidence 4 / 5.

### Summary

The paper argues that a valid vendor signature is not sufficient authorization for a cross-organization agent tool call, and proposes receiver-owned bilateral admission inside the Chio runtime. Before the receiving kernel dispatches a federated request, it resolves treaty scope, participant keys, ladder manifests, continuation, lineage, prior receipts, leases, governance records and revocation state from its own stores (never from request metadata), verifies one canonical DSSE statement (predicate type chio.bilateral-cosign-invocation.v1) signed by two distinct configured keys that binds request, outcome, treaty, continuation, lineage and both receipt digests, consumes the continuation once, and denies with a named code on any missing, stale, replayed or mismatched artifact. Accepted evidence is carried into receipt construction as a typed value (VerifiedFederationTreatyMaterial) so the signed receipt cannot be assembled from a different request. A small Lean 4 model of a bounded receipt-predicate language (nine atoms plus Boolean connectives) proves that a finite-domain refinement checker is exactly the implication quantified over the supplied list; a separately written Rust interpreter is differentially tested against the runtime evaluator on every atom and 3 x 1,024 generated cases. The evaluation reports 20 named negative cases that all deny, a Criterion measurement of the complete pre-dispatch denial path (11.1 ms p50 in the submitted PDF) with a tool-invocation counter asserted zero, a single-host three-vendor SQLite workflow that produces and verifies a 51.8 KB buyer package (2.5 s p50), component microbenchmarks, and a 50-fixture generic replay corpus.

### Strengths

- The design principle is correct and stated without hedging: a signature authenticates bytes, so the receiver must resolve the interpreting context (keys, treaty, revocation, continuation state) from state it controls, and request metadata must never be able to install a trust root. This is the right generalization of hard-won lessons from JWT/JWKS and SAML deployments to agent tool calls.
- The implementation appears to be a real, integrated path rather than a library demo: a kernel hook that runs after ordinary capability checks, a continuation that is reserved during admission, consumed on the admitted path and released on abort, and a typed carrier that couples the accepted admission result to receipt construction so the second binding cannot be reassembled for a different request.
- Using a tool-invocation counter as the oracle for 'denied before dispatch' in a dispatch-capable benchmark is a sound experimental idea, and the paper is careful to separate cases that only reach the envelope verifier from cases that traverse the hook.
- The paper is unusually explicit about what is not established (two configured keys do not prove two organizations; Lean does not verify Rust; the finite domain says nothing about receipts outside it; single-host only). The CLAIM_LEDGER and the content-addressed artifact manifest are good practice that most submissions lack.
- The writing is compact and the sections map cleanly onto research questions; a reader can tell which surface each claim rests on (Table 1).

### Weaknesses

- W1 (Sec 6.1, 6.4, Abstract, CLAIM_LEDGER, supplementary manifest): the paper and its own artifact disagree on every headline number and on the machine. The submitted PDF and the CLAIM_LEDGER report pre-dispatch denial 11.124 ms p50 / 20.530 ms p99, buyer workflow 2.488 s / 3.291 s, receipt sign 234.3 us, strict DSSE verify 436.7 us, offline package verify 50.4 ms, negative matrix 15.7 s, bilateral replay 14 s. The result files pinned by sha256 in artifact-manifest.json and consumed by the LaTeX source (bilateral-admission.json, -components.csv, -inline.tex, replay-corpus-inline.tex) report 4.115 ms / 9.790 ms, 2.370 s / 3.379 s, 134.7 us, 101.1 us, 15.2 ms, 10.7 s and 11 s. The pinned bilateral-admission-environment.txt identifies the host as aarch64-apple-darwin, 'Darwin MacBookPro 25.4.0 ... ARM64_T6000', while Sec 6.1 hard-codes 'Linux/aarch64 with eight Arm Neoverse-N1 cores and 49.2 GB RAM' and the abstract says 'an eight-core Arm server'. The results JSON records commit 3dfd241e; source-commit.txt records dfcd5116; the manifest says generatedAt 2026-07-26 yet pins the August files. The CLAIM_LEDGER sentence 'The retained result files report: 11.124 ms p50' is false for the retained files that ship. Rebuilding the PDF from the pinned sources would produce a Methods section naming a Linux server above numbers measured on a laptop. For a paper whose thesis is that evidence must be bound to the state that interprets it, this is disqualifying for artifact evaluation and must be fixed before any number in the paper can be trusted.
- W2 (Sec 6.1, 6.4, Abstract): the reported 'p99' is not a tail latency. Per the retained run-bilateral-admission.sh, each component observation is a Criterion per-sample mean (elapsed/iters under linear sampling), and the percentile is nearest-rank over 30 such means, so 'p99' is literally the largest of 30 batch averages; for the 20-run workflows it is the single slowest run. Batch means suppress exactly the tail the abstract advertises. Report per-invocation distributions with adequate sample counts, or report min/median/max and confidence intervals and drop the p99 language. The Limitations section concedes the samples cannot support tail targets, but the abstract and conclusion still lead with p99.
- W3 (Sec 6.4): the admitted path is never measured through the kernel. The only end-to-end kernel number is the denial path. The 'cross-boundary admission allow' component (14 to 20 us) cannot include store resolution, strict DSSE verification (101 to 437 us on its own), continuation consumption, dispatch, receipt signing or the SQLite append, so it is not the cost of admitting a federated call. A systems venue needs the overhead of a successful cross-organization call relative to a local call on the same tool, decomposed into resolution, verification, continuation, dispatch and durable receipt, plus throughput under concurrent federated requests. None of this is present.
- W4 (Sec 6.4, Table 3): the headline denial cost is dominated by SQLite durability, not by the protocol. With the PDF numbers, the denial path is 11.1 ms p50 while a bare SQLite receipt append is 7.8 ms p50 (about 70 percent); with the retained numbers, 4.1 ms versus 3.2 ms (about 77 percent). The paper does not decompose the path, does not state journal or synchronous mode, and does not say whether the denial receipt write is fsync-bound. As reported, the number characterizes a SQLite configuration more than receiver-owned admission.
- W5 (Sec 6.2, Table 2, Sec 3.2 'Failure evidence'): RQ1 is regression testing presented as an adversarial evaluation. The retained matrix log shows that the 20 'named negative cases' are 20 of the authors' own pre-existing unit tests (for example step_13_verdict_disagreement_fails_closed, step_14_expired_lease_fails_closed), each executed as 'cargo test <name>' with the expected code supplied through an environment variable; 'both matrix runs' are the debug and release runs of the same tests. There is no attacker model that generates inputs, no fuzzing or property-based testing of the strict verifier, and no mutation evidence that any test would fail if a check were removed. Only 7 of the 20 cases traverse the runtime hook; 13 stop in the envelope verifier without a dispatch-capable surface. Three of the five 'binding substitution' cases (PS-TH-05, -06, -07: substituted remote-receipt, request and outcome hashes) terminate with predicate.schema_invalid, which suggests the mismatch is caught by schema validation rather than by a binding comparison, or that the diagnostic does not distinguish a substituted digest from a malformed document; Table 2 lists these as 'receipt, request, outcome' rejections without explanation. The 10.7 s / 15.7 s matrix time is 20 cargo invocations and is not a measurement of anything.
- W6 (Sec 4, Abstract contribution 3): the formal contribution is thin and does not touch the mechanism the paper is about. finite_refinement_exact states that a Boolean fold of an implication over a list is equal to the universally quantified implication over that list; that is essentially unfolding the definition of the checker. The modeled language (nine atoms, Boolean connectives, finite domain) is explicitly 'not an alternate authorization path' (Sec 3.2), so neither the strict verifier nor the admission hook has any formal statement. The 'independently written' Rust interpreter is not substantiated (same authors? clean-room? shared serde types?), and Rust-vs-Rust differential agreement on generator-produced inputs inside the runtime's limits is weak evidence of anything beyond drift detection. Presenting this as one of four contributions overstates it.
- W7 (Sec 2, Sec 3.2 step 2): the entire protocol is bypassed if a foreign action is classified as local, and the paper places that classification in the trust base ('we assume ... the receiver classifies federated origin correctly'). In an agent setting the obvious attack is a local agent that launders a vendor's request as its own local tool call. Nothing in the paper explains what makes the classification trustworthy, how it interacts with local agents that hold capabilities for the same tools, or whether the negative corpus contains a single misclassification case.
- W8 (Sec 3, Fig 1, Fig 2): the protocol flow is never specified. Who constructs the bilateral predicate, in which order do the two parties sign, and how can an 'outcome digest' be bound in a statement the receiver verifies before the tool runs? If the receiver's own signature is one of the two required signatures, the receiver signs a statement and then verifies its own signature as an input to its own admission decision, which needs explanation. If the outcome is that of a parent action referenced through the continuation, say so and draw the timeline. Figures 1 and 2 are boxes and arrows with no messages, no ordering and no state; neither would let a reader reimplement the protocol. No wire example, no field sizes, and no canonicalization details are given for a construction whose security rests on canonical bytes.
- W9 (Sec 3.1, 5.1, 9): continuation single-use is the paper's replay defense, but its durability and atomicity are undefined. Is reservation atomic under concurrent requests naming the same continuation? Are consumption and the denial or allow receipt written in one transaction? What happens on a crash between consume and dispatch, or between dispatch and receipt append? The Limitations admit crash recovery is untested; for a systems reviewer this is not a limitation but the core of the mechanism, and 'durability depends on the configured store' (CLAIM_LEDGER) is not an answer.
- W10 (Sec 5.5, 6, 9): nothing is deployed and nothing crosses a process or host boundary. The 'three-vendor workflow' is a deterministic single-host loopback in which one harness generates every participant's keys, treaties and stores; 'cross-organization' is therefore simulated within a single trust domain, and the title, abstract and RQ framing do not say so. The paper offers no account of treaty provisioning, out-of-band key and ladder-manifest exchange, revocation-epoch propagation between organizations, clock skew across organizations for freshness checks, or key rotation for treaty participants, all of which determine whether the design is deployable.
- W11 (Sec 8): related work misses the closest prior art on receiver-resolved authorization evidence: Macaroons and Biscuit (contextual and third-party caveats that the verifier discharges against its own state), SPKI/SDSI and proof-carrying authorization, OAuth token exchange and sender-constrained tokens (RFC 8693, DPoP), COSE countersignatures (RFC 9338) as the standard form of 'two signatures over the same bytes', and IETF SCITT transparency receipts, which are the standards-track cross-organization signed-statement primitive. The practice of refusing request-carried trust roots (jku/x5u hardening) should be acknowledged as prior practice rather than presented as new.
- W12 (Sec 6.5 RQ4, Sec 3.1, Sec 7): presentation and scope padding. The 50-fixture 'generic capability corpus' checks stored golden verdicts and has nothing to do with bilateral admission; its inclusion inflates RQ4. Terms such as ladder manifest, enforcement mode, partition-contingency, quorum-required, governance receipt, lease, session anchor and consistency model are used without definition. Roles (buyer, vendor, receiver, sender, participant A/B, local/remote kernel) are not mapped onto one another. The phrase 'programmable sovereignty' appears once, in the Discussion, and only to disclaim itself.

### The one change that would most move the score

## P2: Bilateral Receipt Admission: Cross-Organizational Action Provenance with Treaty-Bound DSSE (docs/papers/bilateral-receipt-admission)

Venue: ACM CCS. Persona: Security PC member with extensive DSSE/in-toto/SCITT reviewing history; checks every gate and code claim for internal consistency. Score 2 / 5, confidence 4 / 5.

### Summary

The paper argues that supply-chain provenance (in-toto, SLSA, Sigstore, SCITT) records how an artifact was produced, whereas cross-organization agent tool calls need a record of how an action was admitted at the receiving runtime ("kernel"). It proposes a DSSE profile, predicate type chio.bilateral-cosign-invocation.v1, in which a single in-toto subject digest is SHA-256 over an RFC 8785 (JCS) encoding of a tuple of hashes: treaty scope, ladder intersection, admission report, continuation, request, outcome, local receipt, remote receipt, lease, and signer key ids. Two kernels each sign the same PAE bytes with Ed25519. A strict verifier, after checking both signatures against a verifier-owned trust store, evaluates six gates (canonical payload, predicate type, exactly two distinct keyids, keyid membership in issuer/kernel allowlists, lease epoch not older than the verifier's revocation epoch, subject digest equals hash of the binding tuple) and emits one of five rejection codes. The paper describes a Rust runtime hook that fails closed on federated requests, a BBS selective-disclosure layer that is presentation-only with Ed25519 authoritative, a three-vendor in-process "buyer closure" that emits signed positive and negative packages, and a Lean 4 module that restates a three-conjunct abstraction of the accept relation (issuer-key membership, kernel-key membership, scope-predicate denotation) with three definitional corollaries. Section 7 claims six attack classes are defeated structurally (sibling-treaty substitution, BBS stub-vs-real, single-lane witness compromise, error-message oracles, schema-version downgrade, and a constitutional ratchet that is explicitly out of scope). The evaluation reports a 72 us local-policy admission benchmark, a treaty-intersection benchmark, and a 50-fixture manifest-stability replay; no measurement of the bilateral verifier itself is reported. Polity-level results are deferred to an anonymized companion paper.

### Strengths

- The framing (admission provenance vs build provenance) is crisp and timely, and the core design instinct is right: the receiver's context (treaty, ladder intersection, continuation, both receipt hashes) belongs inside the signed subject rather than in out-of-band metadata, so that a valid signature cannot be re-interpreted under a different relationship.
- Rejection codes as a protocol-level surface, with signed negative packages carried in the same canonical schema as positive packages (Section 6, 'What the closure demonstrates'), is a genuinely useful auditability design that most attestation profiles omit.
- The verifier-owned trust store principle (Section 5: request-borne trust roots, peer directories, keys, and bundles are adversarial input, never configuration) is stated clearly and is the correct posture for cross-organization admission.
- The authors are unusually candid about the limits of their artifacts: Section 4 ('What the Lean module does not prove') and Section 6 ('Bench scope', 'What is not measured here') explicitly disclaim what a less careful paper would have implied.
- The Ed25519-authoritative / BBS-presentation-only split (Sections 2, 5, 7) is a sound policy stance that avoids the common mistake of letting a selective-disclosure proof become the admission credential.
- The SCITT contrast in Section 8 (two-issuer joint accept at admission time vs issuer-plus-transparency-service countersignature that may post-date the statement) is well drawn and is the right standards-level comparator.

### Weaknesses

- W1 (Sections 3, 6, 7): The definition of the headline gate G6 changes between sections. Section 3 formalizes G6 as payload self-consistency, subjectDigest = H(canonical(E.payload.bindingTuple)), i.e. the subject is compared with a tuple read from the same payload. Sections 6 and 7 instead say the verifier 'reconstructs the subject digest against T_B's scope and ladder-intersection hashes', i.e. against treaty state resolved from the trust store. Only the second definition defeats sibling-treaty substitution. Under the Section 3 definition, the attack Section 3 credits to G6 ('editing the binding tuple's treaty-scope field while leaving the upstream signatures in place') is already defeated by DSSE signature verification over the PAE, because both signatures cover the entire payload including subject and predicate; G6 then contributes nothing against a third-party adversary and only catches a signer who mis-assembled its own envelope. The central novelty claim rests on a gate whose semantics the paper does not fix.
- W2 (Sections 3, 5, 6, 7): The verifier's gate order is stated three incompatible ways. Section 3: 'the verifier never reaches (G1)-(G6) until both bytes-level signatures verify'. Section 6 (tampered-canonical-JSON family): denies on noncanonical-payload 'before any signature check'. Section 7 (schema-version downgrade): rejects on predicate-type-mismatch 'before any signature check'. Section 5: 'every byte-level check fires before the verifier consults state the sender cannot see', yet signature verification requires the trust store's keys. For a paper whose contribution is a strict verifier with an 'evaluated left-to-right' conjunction and an information-leak argument that depends on that order, this is disqualifying until resolved.
- W3 (Section 3, Figure 2 caption): The justification for bundling G4 into predicate-type-mismatch is logically wrong. 'A signer outside both allowlists fails (G2) before (G4) is reached' is false: G2 tests the predicateType string, which an unlisted signer can set correctly. What actually makes G4 redundant is the pre-gate key lookup during signature verification, which the paper elsewhere says happens first. Section 5 then lists 'peer key pinning' as an operational-layer check with no rejection code at all.
- W4 (Sections 3, 5, 6, 7): The five-code taxonomy is claimed to be total ('a total function ... to either one of the five rejection codes or an admission verdict'; 'every disagreement is named by exactly one rejection code') but is not. Section 5's operational layer enumerates ladder-manifest freshness, receipt resolution, request and tool-argument hashes, policy-verdict agreement, governance receipts, and treaty binding identifiers, none of which maps to a code. Section 6 reports a replay denial 'on continuation-hash divergence' and Section 7 a denial on 'the quorum predicate'; neither is one of the five codes, and neither gate appears in G1-G6. Section 5 also says all five codes are produced by verify_chio_bilateral_dsse_envelope, while Table 1 sources two of them from a different file (bilateral_verifier.rs).
- W5 (Section 3, Figure 1, worked envelope): The binding tuple is described four different ways. The opening paragraph lists twelve items including treaty id and a governance receipt reference; the 'Canonical encoding' paragraph lists ten fields with neither; Figure 1 draws nine fields (no admissionRptHash) under a caption that says ten; the worked envelope has eleven predicate keys and no governance reference. How leaseHash and signerKidsHash are derived from the lease object and the kid array (encoding, ordering) is unspecified, and ordering matters because G4 assigns sig_1 to IssuerKeys and sig_2 to KernelKeys while DSSE signatures are an unordered set.
- W6 (Section 3): The wire format is not in-toto or DSSE conformant as shown. in-toto Statement v1 requires _type = https://in-toto.io/Statement/v1 and a TypeURI predicateType; the paper uses 'in-toto-statement/v1' and a bare dotted string. The worked envelope places 'signatures' as a sibling of '_type' and 'predicate', which is neither a DSSE envelope (payloadType, base64 payload, signatures) nor a statement; a reader cannot tell which bytes the PAE covers. More seriously, the DSSE protocol states that KEYID is an 'unauthenticated hint' that 'MUST NOT be used for security decisions', yet G3 and G4 are keyid tests. The paper must either check distinctness and membership of the public keys that actually verified, or state that it deviates from DSSE by resolving keys strictly by keyid. No gate checks that the verified keys equal payload.signerKids, so with several keys in IssuerKeys an envelope naming (opus#1, alpha#1) can be signed by (opus#2, alpha#1) and pass.
- W7 (Section 3): The prose gates and the formal accept set disagree. The signer-reuse prose adds an 'operator-resolution test parameterized by verifier-owned attribution metadata' that is absent from G3 and from the trust store S = (IssuerKeys, KernelKeys, LeaseEpoch); the stale-lease prose adds an expiry check that is absent from G5 and requires a clock S does not contain. Separately, the claim that JCS supplies 'Unicode string normalization' is false: RFC 8785 states that 'JCS-compliant string processing does not take this into consideration' and that components 'MUST preserve Unicode string data as is'. Two implementations that NFC-normalize differently before JCS will produce different canonical bytes, which is exactly the equivocation the paper says G1 closes.
- W8 (Section 4, abstract, Section 8): The Lean artifact is, by the authors' own description, a definitional one-line biconditional over a three-conjunct abstraction that contains no hash, digest, tuple, lease, canonicalization, or signature count. It cannot 'foreclose on accidental drift between the informal schema and the mechanical verifier' because it does not mention the schema's fields. Yet the abstract says correctness 'rests on the conjunction of six gate predicates over canonical bytes, formalized in a Lean module', and Section 8 claims the module 'proves the analogous claim' to Cedar's Rust-vs-Lean soundness theorem, which Section 4 itself denies ('the two artifacts share no formal connection'). The identification of the 'scope-predicate denotation' conjunct with G6 is unmotivated. The abstract counts two kernel axioms; Section 4 counts three.
- W9 (Section 6): The evaluation does not measure the paper's primitive. The 72.051 us figure is the local-policy admission path ('not a cryptographic-primitive measurement'); treaty intersection is a different routine; receipt sign/verify are 'out of scope'; a verifier microbenchmark is 'deferred'. The 50-fixture replay checks manifest stability of stored verdicts, not adversarial re-execution. The 'Denied paths' paragraph claims signer-reuse and stale-lease fixtures exist, while the 'Bilateral-relevant fixture families' paragraph says a corpus covering predicate-type-mismatch, signer-reuse, and stale-lease is 'follow-up work'. Section 9 discloses the three-vendor closure is in-process with a shared clock, which undercuts 'without privileged access to vendor internals'. Single machine (Apple M1 Max), single toolchain, no variance beyond one CI.
- W10 (whole paper): There is no threat model section and no security definition. The paper calls itself a 'cryptographic construction' and 'cryptographic primitive' but never states the adversary's capabilities, the property (e.g. no adversary lacking both named keys can cause accept for treaty tau on a tuple the two kernels did not jointly sign; no accepted envelope is accepted under a different tau), or an argument for it. The cryptographic content is two EUF-CMA signatures over one message plus a hash commitment; the security is inherited. What is new is a context-binding pattern (compare audience claims, channel binding, domain separation) and it should be presented and argued as such.
- W11 (Sections 1, 8): Novelty is overstated against deployed systems. in-toto layouts already require k-of-n functionaries to sign identical link metadata for a step (threshold = 2 is bilateral attestation over identical bytes); DSSE natively carries multiple signatures; COSE_Sign (RFC 9052) is multi-signer; W3C VC Data Integrity has a BBS cryptosuite that is the natural home of the selective-disclosure layer; Biscuit/Macaroons address attenuated cross-org authorization; CCF and SCITT receipts are governed multi-member ledgers. The sentence 'No deployed primitive attests that two parties jointly admitted an action ... over identical canonical bytes' does not survive in-toto thresholds. None of these is discussed.
- W12 (Section 3, G5; Section 6): G5 compares lease.epoch against a single verifier-wide LeaseEpoch. Every revocation that bumps the epoch retroactively renders every earlier honest envelope 'stale', which contradicts the Section 6 claim that 'the buyer replays the canonical envelope and recovers either the same admission or the same rejection code' unless trust-store snapshots are part of the audit format, which the paper does not specify. Intra-lease replay is explicitly not prevented by the verifier, yet Section 6 credits a replay denial to the verifier.
- W13 (Sections 3, 7, 1): The oracle arithmetic is off and the claim is walked back. There are seven distinguishable outcomes (accept, five codes, pre-gate signature failure), so the per-attempt leak bound is log2 7 (about 2.81 bits), not log2 5. Section 3 concedes adaptive conjunct localization in O(N) probes with non-uniform gate timing, while the introduction and Section 7 say error-message oracles are 'rejected by construction'.
- W14 (Sections 2, 5, 7): The BBS layer is unspecified where it matters. How a BBS presentation is bound to a specific Ed25519-signed record (does the BBS message vector include the receipt hash? who is the BBS issuer relative to the two kernels?) is never stated, so the 'stub-versus-real' defense is an assertion. 'BBS group signatures' conflates Boneh-Boyen-Shacham group signatures with the IETF BBS multi-message signature draft. 'The predicate type pins which signature is authoritative' names a string, not a mechanism.
- W15 (throughout): The paper defers at least eight load-bearing items to an anonymized companion (intra-lease replay, schema rotation, PQC migration, cross-process federation, anchor lanes, the ten-family corpus, receipt latencies, amendment refinement). What remains self-standing is a two-signature DSSE profile, five error codes, a definitional Lean lemma, and benchmarks of other components. The macOS Endpoint Security stub paragraph in Section 5 is unrelated to the construction and reads as repository status rather than paper content.
- W16 (minor, presentation): 'Three load-bearing limits' introduces six paragraphs; attack-class counts are three (abstract), four (introduction), and six (Section 7); Section 6 cites 'the multi-lane policy of Section 3', which contains none; source line numbers (bilateral_dsse.rs:996) are unstable identifiers in a paper; Section 9 says the wire format 'does not currently version-tag' while the predicate type ends in '.v1' and the subject digest map is keyed by algorithm name.

## P2: Bilateral Receipt Admission: Cross-Organizational Action Provenance with Treaty-Bound DSSE

Venue: IEEE S&P. Persona: Formal-methods reviewer (reads the Lean description critically; asks whether a definitional bi-conditional is a contribution). Score 2 / 5, confidence 4 / 5.

### Summary

The paper proposes a DSSE/in-toto predicate type, chio.bilateral-cosign-invocation.v1, for recording that two organizational "kernels" (runtime admission engines) jointly admitted a cross-organization agent tool call. The statement's single subject digest is SHA-256 over an RFC 8785 (JCS) encoding of a tuple of hashes (treaty scope, ladder intersection, admission report, continuation, request, outcome, local receipt, remote receipt, lease, signer key ids), and the envelope carries exactly two Ed25519 signatures under distinct key ids over the same payload bytes. A verifier evaluates six ordered gates (payload canonical, predicate type strict, exactly two distinct signature slices, trust-store membership, lease epoch not older than the verifier's revocation epoch, subject digest recomputes) and returns one of five rejection codes (noncanonical-payload, predicate-type-mismatch, signer-reuse, stale-lease, subject-digest-mismatch). The paper describes a Rust runtime with a fail-closed pre-dispatch admission hook that refuses request-carried trust roots, a strict verifier split into an "envelope layer" and an "operational layer", a BBS selective-disclosure module that is presentation-only, and a three-vendor buyer closure with one admitted path and three denied fixtures. Reported measurements are a 72 us local admission-hook latency on an M1 Max (explicitly excluding DSSE verification), a 50-fixture generic capability replay corpus checked for manifest stability, and a ladder-intersection scaling bench at N = 1, 10, 100. Section 7 lists six attack classes said to be rejected by construction. Section 4 presents a Lean 4 module whose main theorem, freestanding_accept_set_theorem, states that a three-conjunct accept predicate (issuer-key membership, kernel-key membership, scope-predicate denotation) is equivalent to its own definition, with three corollaries obtained by unfolding it; the paper itself states the bi-conditional is definitional and proved by one tactic line. Polity-level claims (amendments, treaty intersection, multi-lane anchoring, schema versioning, intra-lease replay, PQC migration) are deferred to an anonymized companion manuscript.

### Strengths

- The problem is real and well framed: SLSA, in-toto, Sigstore and Rekor attest how an artifact was produced; nothing in the deployed ecosystem attests that a receiving organization admitted a cross-organization agent action under a jointly agreed predicate set. Casting that record as an in-toto predicate type with a deterministic single subject digest is the right shape for composing with SCITT, Rekor and COSE receipts, and Section 8's contrast between a two-issuer joint accept and SCITT's issuer-plus-witness pair is a genuinely useful structural observation.
- Making the rejection taxonomy part of the protocol surface rather than debugging output (Section 3, Section 5) is a sound design principle; an auditor who can replay canonical bytes and recover the same rejection code as the original verifier is a property worth having, and the paper articulates the remediation-differs-per-code rationale clearly.
- The verifier-owned trust store principle (Section 5: request-borne trust roots, peer directories, signing keys and dynamic trust bundles are adversarial input, not configuration) is correct, crisply stated, and is the part of the design that actually carries the security argument.
- The paper is unusually candid about its own limits. Section 4 says outright that the Lean theorem is not deep, not load-bearing, and shares no formal connection with the Rust verifier; Section 6 says the primitive itself is not benchmarked and that three of five rejection codes have no fixture; Section 9 names single-vendor key custody, in-process-only federation, side channels and the malicious receiver. Reviewers can trust the scoping statements.
- Ed25519-over-canonical-bytes as authoritative with BBS restricted to presentation (Section 2, Section 5) is the correct policy for an audit corpus and avoids the common mistake of letting a selective-disclosure proof stand in for the signed record.

### Weaknesses

- W1 (Section 4, Abstract, Section 1 item 5, Section 8). The Lean contribution is a definitional tautology. freestanding_accept_set_theorem states accept(S,E) iff [the three conjuncts accept is defined as]; the authors say so themselves ('the bi-conditional is definitional ... one Lean tactic line that unfolds the definition'). The three corollaries are List-membership monotonicity, the denotation of Boolean conjunction, and a contrapositive, all obtained by unfolding. The 'kernel-only axioms' paragraph concedes this is a hygiene property every sorry-free development has. None of this is a result; it is the statement that a definition equals itself. Yet it is listed as one of five contributions, headlined in the abstract ('formalized in a Lean module whose only kernel axioms are ...'), and in Section 8 is presented as the analogue of Cedar's soundness theorem, which it is not (Cedar proves properties of the evaluator and bridges to Rust by differential testing; nothing here does either).
- W2 (Sections 3, 4, 5). There are three different verifiers in the paper and no refinement between any pair. The abstract and Section 1 give five conditions; Section 3 gives six gates over a trust store S = (IssuerKeys, KernelKeys, LeaseEpoch); Section 4 gives three conjuncts over an envelope with fields issuerSig, kernelSig, scope, receiptId that do not exist in the Section 3 wire format; Section 5 describes an envelope layer plus an operational layer with roughly a dozen checks (receipt resolution, request and tool-argument hashes, policy verdict agreement, governance receipts, treaty binding identifiers, ladder-manifest freshness) that appear in none of the formal statements. The paper's stated purpose for the Lean module is to 'foreclose on accidental drift between the informal schema and the mechanical verifier'; the drift has already occurred inside the submission.
- W3 (Section 4). The Lean abstraction drops signer distinctness (G3), and the paper's justification ('the trust store names keys whose ... signer-distinctness checks the deployed verifier has separately discharged') is a category error: distinctness is a relation between two signature slices, not a property of any key, so it cannot be encoded as trust-store membership. Consequently, whenever issuerKeys and kernelKeys overlap (which they must if the two cosigners are symmetric kernels, as the abstract and Figure 1 say), the Lean accept set contains envelopes where both slices carry the same key id. That is precisely the confused-deputy cosignature Section 3 claims G3 defeats. The mechanized object admits the attack the prose says is impossible.
- W4 (Abstract, Section 1, Section 3). The 'iff' characterization of the verifier is false for the implemented verifier. Section 5 states that envelopes clearing the six gates are further gated on receipt resolution, request hashes, policy verdict agreement, governance receipts and treaty identifiers; Section 6 states that the replay-attack family denies on 'continuation-hash divergence' and that receipts fail 'the quorum predicate' over anchor lanes. None of these is a gate in accept(S,E). The six gates are at most necessary conditions, so every occurrence of 'iff' should be 'only if', and the five-code taxonomy cannot be complete for the deployed verifier.
- W5 (Section 3 G6; Section 6 'Denied paths'; Section 7). G6 as formalized, E.subjectDigest = H(canonical(E.payload.bindingTuple)), takes both sides from E and is an internal consistency check inside the signed payload. Since both signatures cover the payload bytes and (per Figure 1) the binding tuple is inside the payload, any edit to treaty-scope or ladder-intersection fields breaks both signatures at the pre-gate check and never reaches G6. The sibling-treaty substitution the paper says G6 defeats is described in Sections 6 and 7 as 'the verifier reconstructs the subject digest against T_B's scope and ladder-intersection hashes', which is a comparison against receiver-resolved treaty state. That comparison is not in accept(S,E): S contains no treaty manifests. The formalized gate does not express the security property claimed for it.
- W6 (Sections 3, 5, 6, 7). Gate ordering relative to signature verification is contradictory. Section 3: 'the verifier never reaches (G1)-(G6) until both bytes-level signatures verify'. Section 6: tampered canonical JSON 'denies on noncanonical-payload before any signature check'. Section 7: schema downgrade 'rejects on predicate-type-mismatch before any signature check'. Section 5 enumerates the envelope-layer checks first and never says where signature verification sits. The oracle analysis in Section 3 assumes post-signature gating; if the Sections 6 and 7 ordering is correct, an adversary with no key at all obtains R1, R2 and R3 as free distinguishers, and the 'error surface' argument must be redone.
- W7 (Section 3). The binding tuple, which the paper calls its load-bearing artifact, is specified inconsistently four times within one section: the opening paragraph lists twelve items (including treaty id and a governance receipt reference); Figure 1 lists nine (no admission-report hash); the JCS paragraph lists ten with the names leaseHash and signerKidsHash (no treatyId, no governance reference); the worked envelope's predicate has eleven keys, with lease as a structured object and signerKids as an array. The preimages of leaseHash and signerKidsHash are never defined (byte encoding, whether the kid array is order-sensitive). It is therefore not possible to implement the digest from the paper, which defeats the paper's own stated goal that 'the wire format is the load-bearing artifact'.
- W8 (Section 3, Figure 2). The reason given for G4 having no rejection code is a non-sequitur: 'a signer outside both allowlists fails (G2) before (G4) is reached whenever the predicate type is the strict Chio profile'. G2 tests the predicateType string in the payload, which is independent of who signed; an unknown signer with the correct predicate type passes G2 and reaches G4. If unknown key ids in fact fail earlier at key resolution during signature verification, then G4 is redundant with the pre-gate check, and the outcome for an unknown signer is the un-coded signature failure, not R2 as Figure 2's caption says.
- W9 (whole paper). No security definition and no reduction. The paper calls the construction a 'cryptographic primitive' and says 'correctness rests on the conjunction of six gate predicates', but never states what property an accepted envelope has (for instance: no adversary lacking at least one of the two named private keys can produce an envelope accepted under S whose binding tuple neither honest cosigner signed) and never proves it, although the proof is a short reduction to EUF-CMA of Ed25519 plus collision resistance of SHA-256 and injectivity of JCS on the value domain. Without the definition the paper cannot answer the obvious question of what the subject digest adds over two signatures on a payload that already contains every bound field. As far as this reviewer can tell the digest is a citation handle for anchoring, not a security mechanism, and the paper should say so.
- W10 (Sections 2, 3, 6). There is no protocol. The paper never gives a message sequence: who assembles the envelope, in what order the two kernels sign, whether the receiving kernel signs before or after it runs the verifier, and how a pre-dispatch admission hook (Section 6, admitted path: 'evaluates the six-gate verifier ... and admits the action') can verify an envelope whose binding tuple contains the outcome hash and both receipt hashes, which exist only after execution. The cosigner-as-verifier role (receiving kernel, Section 5) and the third-party replay role (buyer, Section 6) are conflated into a single accept(S,E) although they have different trust stores and different properties.
- W11 (Section 6). The evaluation does not measure the primitive. The 72 us figure is the local-policy hook and, by the paper's own 'Bench scope' paragraph, excludes the two Ed25519 verifications and the binding-tuple hash; the 50-fixture corpus is the generic kernel-capability golden target and checks 'manifest stability across machines rather than re-execution of the kernel'; the treaty-intersection bench measures a different routine. The 'Denied paths' paragraph says signer-reuse and stale-lease fixtures exist in the closure, while the 'Bilateral-relevant fixture families' paragraph says a corpus covering predicate-type-mismatch, signer-reuse and stale-lease 'is named as follow-up work'. Either way, at least one and possibly three of the five rejection codes have no reported test, and the sentence 'The measurements above bound the cryptographic-primitive surface' is not true.
- W12 (Section 7 vs Section 3; Abstract). Section 7 lists error-message oracles among attacks 'rejected structurally'; Section 3 concedes an adaptive adversary can localize the failing sub-field in O(N) probes and that 'adaptive bounds against the gate-ordering oracle remain open'. The abstract repeats the Section 7 claim. Of the remaining five classes, single-lane witness compromise depends on an anchor quorum predicate that is not among the gates and whose 'multi-lane policy of Section 3' does not exist in Section 3; constitutional ratchet is explicitly out of scope; schema-version downgrade is a restatement of G2; BBS stub-versus-real is a policy statement ('the predicate type pins which signature is authoritative') with no corresponding gate. The catalogue therefore contains one open problem, two out-of-scope items, one restatement, and one unverified policy.
- W13 (Sections 1, 3, 6, 7, 8, 9). The paper is not self-contained. At least nine substantive items are deferred to an anonymized companion: intra-lease replay, schema-version rotation, multi-lane witness policy, receipt sign and verify latency, the full ten-family corpus, cross-process federation, PQC and cipher-suite agility, amendment refinement, and polity-level admission. The named companion theorems (amendment_admissible_iff_backward_refinement, treaty_admission_iff_predicate_intersection, essential_preserved_chain) cannot be checked. If the companion is the receiver-owned bilateral admission manuscript circulating under the same predicate type, the runtime hook, the strict verifier function and the three-vendor buyer closure appear to be the same artifacts claimed as contributions 2 and 3 here; the PC should verify the contribution boundary between the two submissions.
- W14 (miscellaneous technical inaccuracies). RFC 8785 does not perform 'Unicode string normalization' (it specifies ES6-style string serialization and UTF-16 key ordering; NFC is not applied). BBS as in the CFRG draft is a multi-message signature scheme supporting selective disclosure, not a group signature in the BBS04 sense. The 'log2 5 = 2.32 bits' bound counts five outcomes; the verifier has at least six (five codes plus accept) and seven once the un-coded signature failure is counted. The abstract lists two Lean axioms, Section 4 lists three. G5 tests only epoch although the stale-lease prose includes expiry. The sentence 'the comparison reads the verifier's epoch, not any field in the envelope' contradicts G5, which reads E.payload.lease.epoch. Section 9 announces 'three load-bearing limits' and lists six. Section 9's 'constant-time idealization' contradicts Section 3's deliberately ordered, code-distinguished gates. Section 5's threat model names 'an arbitrary network adversary' while Section 9 says there is no network.

### Detailed comments

- Abstract. 'A strict verifier ... accepts the envelope iff (i)-(v)' and 'correctness rests on the conjunction of six gate predicates over canonical bytes, formalized in a Lean module'. The Lean module formalizes three conjuncts, not six, over a different record type, and Section 4 itself calls it 'a structural projection, not a runtime equivalence'. The abstract should not claim the six-gate conjunction is formalized. Also 'defeats ... error-message oracles' contradicts Section 3's open-problem statement.
- Section 1, contribution 5. 'collapse the runtime's six-gate conjunction ... into a structural form Lean can mechanically check'. The collapse loses G1, G2, G3 and G5 and replaces G6 with a scope-denotation over a receiptId that is not a wire-format field. A mechanical check of a different predicate is not a check of this one. Please either model the actual gate list or drop the contribution.
- Section 2. 'The four concerns compose ... the verifier's rejection codes are independent of all three.' No composition argument is given, and the paper's own Section 5 says gate ordering is load-bearing, which is the opposite of independence. Also 'BBS group signatures' is the wrong object; the CFRG draft is not a group signature scheme.
- Section 3, first paragraph vs Figure 1 vs JCS paragraph vs worked envelope. Please produce one normative table: field name in the predicate, type, whether it is in the binding object, the exact preimage bytes for any derived hash (leaseHash, signerKidsHash), and whether array order is significant. State explicitly whether treatyId and the governance receipt reference are in the digest. Right now four descriptions disagree.
- Section 3, canonical encoding. The claim that JCS 'supplies field-tag domain separation by construction' is fine, but the paper should state the actual assumptions it needs: JCS is injective on the I-JSON value domain, the verifier's parser is a left inverse of JCS (rejects duplicate keys, does not lose precision on numbers), and no field value may be attacker-chosen in a way that changes structure. 'Unicode string normalization' is not something RFC 8785 does.
- Section 3, G1. The presentation-attack rationale (different verifiers re-canonicalize and diverge) is handled by signature verification over presented bytes, not by G1. G1's actual value is that the payload hash is unique per semantic content so that citations (continuation hash, receipt-graph edges, anchors) are well defined. Say that.
- Section 3, G3 signer-reuse prose. 'distinct keyids that resolve to the same operator under verifier-owned attribution metadata' is not part of G3 as formalized (kid1 != kid2), is not in Table 1 (require_unique_signature_keyids is the trivial check), and Section 9 concedes single-vendor custody is not detected. Please remove the operator-resolution claim or formalize and test it.
- Section 3, G4. Which signature slice is the 'issuer' and which the 'kernel'? DSSE signatures are an unordered list. If the check is positional, an adversary swapping the slices changes the verdict; if IssuerKeys and KernelKeys are the same set for symmetric cosigners, G4 adds nothing to G3 plus signature verification. The Lean model's asymmetric issuerSig/kernelSig fields inherit this ambiguity.
- Section 3, G5. The gate compares an envelope field to verifier state; that is fine, but the prose says the comparison reads 'not any field in the envelope'. Also: if both kernels sign, a stale epoch means both honest kernels signed a stale epoch. What adversary is G5 aimed at? The post-revocation replay story requires that the envelope was valid when minted and the epoch advanced afterwards; then G5 is a revocation check, and the paper should say what revokes (a lease? a key? a treaty?) and who advances S.LeaseEpoch.
- Section 3, G6. Please restate the gate so that at least one side comes from S (resolved treaty scope hash, ladder-intersection hash, expected continuation). As written G6 can only fail if a cosigner signed an inconsistent payload, which is not the attack the paper describes.
- Section 3, taxonomy paragraph. 'A signature-bytes failure denies the envelope before the six gates are evaluated' is incompatible with Section 6's and Section 7's 'before any signature check' for R1 and R2. Please give one ordered list and use it consistently; then redo the oracle analysis for the unauthenticated adversary.
- Section 4, opening. 'a bilateral envelope E carrying a receipt id, a scope predicate, and two signature slices' is not the Section 3 envelope. If the Lean E is an abstraction of the wire E, give the abstraction function and state which wire-format gates it preserves. The reviewer's reading is that it preserves none of G1, G2, G3, G5 and replaces G6 with something else.
- Section 4, corollary 2. The sentence 'stated without reusing the original signatures for a byte-distinct rebound envelope' is meaningless in context (the corollary is about the denotation of p AND q) and appears to be spliced from elsewhere. Please fix.
- Section 4, 'What the Lean module does not prove'. This paragraph is accurate and, taken together with the first paragraph, amounts to the authors agreeing that the module proves nothing about the system. A formal-methods reader will ask: what would a non-trivial theorem look like here? Candidates that would be meaningful and cheap: (a) determinism and totality of the rejection code function, with each rejected envelope receiving exactly the code of the first failing gate; (b) a binding lemma: accept(S,E), accept(S,E'), equal subject digests implies equal binding tuples up to hash collision; (c) preservation of signer distinctness under the abstraction; (d) a Cedar-style differential harness between the Lean decision procedure and the Rust verifier on a shared corpus.
- Section 5, strict bilateral verifier. Signature verification is not listed in either the envelope layer or the operational layer. Where is it? Also 'canonical-JSON equivalence between the inner payload and its declared digest': what is a payload's declared digest? This may be the actual G6, in which case the Section 3 statement should match.
- Section 5, Table 1. Three of five codes map to the same source line (bilateral_dsse.rs:996). Line numbers are brittle and uninformative for review; name symbols. The table also places stale-lease and subject-digest-mismatch in the operational verifier, which confirms that the Section 3 'six gates in one function' picture is not the implementation.
- Section 5, stub disclosure. The macOS Endpoint Security paragraph is irrelevant to the primitive by the paper's own admission and reads as filler; the more interesting stub question is whether the BBS module is a stub (the attack name 'BBS stub-versus-real' suggests one exists). Please clarify.
- Section 6, admitted path. 'consumes the continuation before dispatch, evaluates the six-gate verifier ... and admits the action' while the envelope binds outcome and both receipt hashes. Please give a sequence diagram or timeline; as written the pre-dispatch verification and the post-execution binding cannot both be true of the same envelope.
- Section 6, dispatch latency and bench scope. The number is honestly scoped, but a verifier-only microbenchmark of two Ed25519 verifications plus one SHA-256 over a few hundred bytes takes minutes to write and would be the only measurement in the paper that concerns the claimed contribution. Its absence is hard to justify.
- Section 6, replay corpus. 'manifest stability across machines rather than re-execution of the kernel on adversarial input' means the reported 50/50 is a hash comparison of stored verdicts, not a test that the verifier rejects anything. Please state what is executed and what is compared.
- Section 6, 'the multi-lane policy of Section 3'. Section 3 contains no multi-lane policy. Dangling reference.
- Section 7, single-lane witness compromise. The anchor-lane quorum is not a gate of the verifier in Section 3, is unmeasured (Section 6), and belongs to the deferred companion. It should not be listed as defeated by this construction.
- Section 8, Cedar. The analogy is overstated: Cedar proves that Rust decisions are derivable from the Lean semantics via a differential harness and proves language-level properties (e.g., forbid-overrides-permit). This paper's Lean module proves a definition equals itself and has no bridge to Rust. Please describe the relation to Cedar accurately (aspiration, not analogue).
- Section 8, Cremers et al. 'the digest commitment substitutes for the symbolic disjointness condition' is an unproven claim about composition; no composition theorem is stated or proved here. Remove or prove.
- Section 9. 'Three load-bearing limits' followed by six paragraphs. 'The strict verifier is treated as a constant-time idealization' contradicts the explicitly ordered gates with distinguishable codes, which are by design not constant-time. 'Malicious receiving kernel' is the right limitation to name; it should be in a threat-model section near the front, not the last page.
- Overlap note for the PC. The reviewer was given the companion receiver-owned bilateral admission manuscript for context. It uses the same predicate type, the same pre-dispatch hook, the same verify_chio_bilateral_dsse_envelope symbol, and the same three-vendor buyer closure, and its evaluation of that shared surface (20 named negative cases, bilateral-verify microbenchmark) is strictly stronger than what P2 reports. If both are under review, contributions 2 and 3 of P2 are not new, and the reviewers should be told which paper owns which artifact.

- Q2. Both sides of G6 come from the envelope. Under what adversary can G6 fail while both signatures verify? If the answer is 'a cosigner that signed an inconsistent payload', then what gate (with S on one side) actually defeats sibling-treaty substitution, and why is it not in accept(S,E)?

### The one change that would most move the score

Make the formal object and the implemented verifier the same object, and prove something non-definitional about it. Concretely: publish one normative gate list that matches the shipped verifier (including the operational-layer checks that compare envelope fields against receiver-resolved treaty state, and the exact position of signature verification), model that list in Lean over the actual wire-format record (not a three-field abstraction that drops signer distinctness), prove at least one property with content (each rejected envelope receives exactly one code naming the first failing gate; accepted envelopes with equal subject digests bind equal tuples up to collision; signer distinctness is preserved by the abstraction), connect the Lean decision procedure to the Rust verifier by a differential corpus that exercises all five rejection codes, and report a verifier-only microbenchmark. ## P2: "Bilateral Receipt Admission: Cross-Organizational Action Provenance with Treaty-Bound DSSE" (ACM format, 10 pages, sections 01-09)

Venue: Industry practitioner review (platform security lead at a large company). Persona: Would-I-deploy-this reader: cares about operational clarity, error taxonomy, migration, and whether the paper describes real shipped behavior. Score 2 / 5, confidence 4 / 5.

### Summary

The paper proposes a DSSE predicate type, chio.bilateral-cosign-invocation.v1, for recording that two organizations' runtime admission engines ("kernels") jointly admitted a cross-organization agent tool call. The payload commits, through a SHA-256 over an RFC 8785 (JCS) canonicalized binding object, to a tuple of hashes covering treaty scope, ladder intersection, admission report, continuation, request, outcome, the two parties' receipt bodies, a capability lease, and the two signer identifiers. The envelope carries exactly two Ed25519 signatures over identical payload bytes from distinct keys pinned in a verifier-owned trust store. A verifier evaluates six ordered gates (canonical payload, predicate-type equality, two distinct signers, trust-store membership, lease epoch freshness, subject-digest consistency) and returns one of five rejection codes so that an auditor can tell which precondition failed. The construction is implemented in Rust behind a pre-dispatch admission hook that denies federated-origin requests lacking treaty context, refuses request-carried trust roots, and releases reserved continuation state on denial. BBS is used for presentation-time selective disclosure with Ed25519 remaining authoritative. A Lean 4 module restates the accept relation as a three-conjunct definition and proves the biconditional by unfolding, with three definitional corollaries. Evaluation consists of a three-vendor closure with one admitted and three denied fixtures, a 72 microsecond benchmark of the local-policy path, a 50-fixture generic capability corpus, and treaty-intersection timings. Replay deduplication, schema versioning, post-quantum migration, multi-lane witness policy, and the full evaluation are deferred to an anonymized companion submission.

### Strengths

- The problem is real and well isolated: provenance of admission at the receiving side is distinct from build provenance, and the SCITT contrast in Section 8 (single issuer plus asynchronous witness versus two issuers jointly signing at admission time, with the cosignature being the admission event) is the clearest and most useful paragraph in the paper.
- The default posture is right for a platform team: federated origin without treaty context denies, request metadata cannot nominate trust roots or key material, and reserved continuation state is released on denial or abort (Section 5). These are stated as structural properties rather than operator-toggleable checks.
- Rejection codes are placed on the protocol surface rather than in debug output, and Section 3 ties each code to a named attack class and an implied remediation. That is the shape an audit consumer and an on-call engineer both need.
- The canonicalization choice is defensible and argued rather than assumed: JCS for the binding object, explicit rejection of raw or length-prefixed concatenation, and reject-rather-than-recanonicalize at the verifier to close the presentation-attacker channel (Section 3).
- Denials are emitted as first-class signed artifacts in the same canonical schema as admissions (Section 6), which is what makes a buyer-side replay audit possible without vendor internals.
- Sections 4 and 9 are unusually candid: the Lean theorem is declared not load-bearing, cross-process federation is not built, single custody domain collapses the bilateral property, side channels are not addressed, and the wire format has no cipher-suite version tag. A deployer knows where the gaps are.

### Weaknesses

- W1 (Section 3, abstract, Figure 1): The wire format, which the paper itself calls the load-bearing artifact, is specified inconsistently. The abstract binds seven items; the opening of Section 3 lists twelve predicate fields; the 'binding tuple' paragraph and Figure 1 caption say ten fields; Figure 1 draws nine (admissionRptHash is missing); the 'canonical encoding' paragraph lists ten, two of which (leaseHash, signerKidsHash) are hashes of structures whose canonical preimage is never defined. treatyId and the governance receipt reference appear in the predicate but not in the binding object, so they are covered by the signatures but not by the subject digest, and the paper does not say whether that is intentional. An implementer cannot build an interoperable verifier from this text.
- W2 (Section 3, worked envelope): The 'worked envelope' is not a DSSE envelope. DSSE is {payloadType, payload (base64), signatures}; the example places signatures inside the in-toto statement object, uses _type 'in-toto-statement/v1' rather than the in-toto v1 statement type URI, and uses a non-URI predicateType contrary to the in-toto attestation framework the paper cites. The signature slots carry 'kid:opus#1' while the predicate carries 'signer kernel ids', and the mapping between them is unstated. For a paper whose contribution is a wire format, these are correctness defects, not typos.
- W3 (Section 3, gates G3/G4 and Section 4): The verifier is position-dependent. G4 requires sig_1.kid in IssuerKeys and sig_2.kid in KernelKeys, so the order of the signatures array is semantically significant, which DSSE does not guarantee and the paper never states. The narrative says both parties are kernels, yet the trust store has separate issuer and kernel allowlists, and nothing prevents one key from being in both lists, in which case the 'independence' requirement reduces to kid inequality. The 'operator-resolution test' against attribution metadata described under signer-reuse appears in no gate, no trust-store definition, no table, and no evaluation.
- W4 (Section 3, G6 and the sibling-treaty argument; Section 6; Section 7): G6 compares subjectDigest to H(canonical(payload.bindingTuple)), but both sides live inside the signed payload. The sibling-treaty attack as described in Section 3 (edit the treaty-scope field, keep the upstream signatures) is defeated by signature verification, not by G6; G6 can only catch a signer that produced a self-inconsistent envelope or an implementation bug. The defense that actually works, described in Sections 6 and 7, compares the envelope's committed scope and ladder hashes against the treaty resolved from the verifier's trust store. That comparison is absent from the six-gate accept predicate. As written, the formal accept relation admits any self-consistent envelope signed by any pinned issuer key plus any pinned kernel key with a fresh lease, under any treaty.
- W5 (Section 3 vs Sections 5, 6, 7): The gate order contradicts itself. Section 3 says the verifier never reaches G1-G6 until both signatures verify; Section 6 says the tampered-canonical-JSON family denies 'before any signature check'; Section 7 says schema-version downgrade rejects on predicate-type-mismatch 'before any signature check'; Section 5 says byte-level checks fire before state is consulted. Whether canonicalization and predicate-type are checked pre- or post-signature changes what an unauthenticated attacker can learn and what code they receive. A deployer needs one authoritative order.
- W6 (Sections 3 and 5, Table 1): The five-code taxonomy is not the shipped error surface. Section 5 describes verify_chio_bilateral_dsse_envelope as 'a total function ... to either one of the five rejection codes or an admission verdict', then lists operational-layer checks (peer key pinning, ladder-manifest freshness, revocation epoch, receipt resolution, request and tool-argument hashes, policy verdict agreement, governance receipts, treaty binding identifiers) that map to no code, and Table 1 places two of the five codes in a different file (bilateral_verifier.rs) from the function said to return them. The pre-gate signature failure is explicitly outside the taxonomy, so the function is not total onto the five codes. The justification for bundling G4 into predicate-type-mismatch (an unpinned signer 'fails G2 before G4') is a non sequitur, since predicateType is attacker-controlled bytes.
- W7 (Section 6): The evaluation does not measure the primitive. The 72 microsecond figure is the local-policy allow path that explicitly excludes the DSSE verify, and the text then calls it 'an upper bound on the bilateral-DSSE cost', which is logically inverted (a path that omits the verify is a lower bound on the composed cost). The 50-fixture corpus is a generic kernel-capability corpus whose reported property is manifest stability, not verifier behavior. The three denied fixtures cover three of the five codes; the paper concedes fixtures for predicate-type-mismatch, signer-reuse, and stale-lease are 'follow-up work' even though signer-reuse and stale-lease are among the three fixtures described two paragraphs earlier. No counts, no pass/fail table, no fixture identifiers, no artifact link, no commit hash. The treaty-intersection routine that produces the only scaling numbers is never described. The 'multi-lane policy of Section 3' referenced here does not exist in Section 3.
- W8 (Section 7): 'Six classes of attack rejected structurally' overcounts. Single-lane witness compromise depends on anchor lanes and a quorum predicate that appear nowhere in the gates, binding tuple, trust store, or implementation. Constitutional ratchet is declared out of scope in the same paragraph. Schema-version downgrade is predicate-type-mismatch restated, and Section 9 admits there is no version tag. Error-message oracle is claimed as defeated even though Section 3 concludes an adaptive adversary localizes the failing sub-field in O(N) probes and that uniform-time evaluation is not provided. That leaves sibling-treaty substitution and confused-deputy cosignature, and W4 shows the first is carried by a check outside the formal predicate.
- W9 (Sections 1, 4): The Lean contribution is oversold in the abstract and introduction relative to Section 4's own account. The abstract says correctness 'rests on the conjunction of six gate predicates ... formalized in a Lean module'; Section 4 says the theorem is definitional, one tactic line, three abstract conjuncts, four of six gates assumed sound, 'not load-bearing', 'a structural projection, not a runtime equivalence'. The Lean envelope carries a receiptId and a scope predicate, neither of which appears in the Section 3 schema, so the claim that the module 'keeps the gate's mechanical statement aligned with the schema' is not supported. A definitional unfolding is not a 'falsifiable artifact'. Corollary 2's sentence ('stated without reusing the original signatures for a byte-distinct rebound envelope') is unintelligible.
- W10 (Section 3, G5; Section 9): Lease semantics are underspecified in ways that matter operationally. G5 compares only lease.epoch against a single verifier epoch; the prose also mentions expiry, which needs a clock that is not in the trust store. A single global monotone epoch means revoking one capability invalidates every outstanding lease from every counterparty (the paper says per-action revocation entries are not retained), which is an availability hazard a deployer must know about. Leases from a future epoch are accepted. Intra-lease replay is explicitly not prevented by the primitive and is deferred to the companion paper, yet Section 6 says replay 'denies on continuation-hash divergence', which is another trust-store comparison missing from the formal gates.
- W11 (Sections 5 and 9): The central property, that two signatures come from two independent operators, is not established by the construction, and the paper is inconsistent about whether it tries. Section 3 claims the signer-reuse gate catches one operator running two kernels via attribution metadata; Section 5 says a single actor controlling both keys 'sits outside the threat model'; Section 9 says operational discipline carries the claim. Pick one and state it in the threat model.
- W12 (Sections 2, 5, 7): Selective disclosure is asserted, not designed. 'BBS group signatures' is a misnomer for the CFRG BBS multi-message scheme. The message-vector layout, the binding between disclosed BBS attributes and the Ed25519-authoritative record, and how a relying party checks a disclosed subset without seeing the full record (which would defeat the purpose) are all absent. The 'BBS stub-versus-real' attack reduces to 'BBS is not accepted for admission', which is a policy choice, not a defeated attack.
- W13 (Throughout): The paper leans on an unseen companion submission for replay deduplication, schema rotation, PQ migration, multi-lane witness policy, amendment, and the full ten-family evaluation (a dozen 'anonymized for review' deferrals) while claiming the primitive is self-standing. Migration is a first-order concern for a platform team: strict predicate-type equality plus no version tag means a v1-to-v2 rollout between two organizations is a flag day, and the paper offers nothing but a pointer. Separately, anonymization is nominal: the predicate string, affiliation, crate paths, and Lean module paths all name the project, and line-number source citations (bilateral_dsse.rs:996) are brittle and unverifiable without an artifact.

### Detailed comments

- Abstract: the bound tuple is enumerated as seven members here, twelve in the opening of Section 3, ten in the 'binding tuple' and 'canonical encoding' paragraphs, and nine in Figure 1. Also 'two named kernel keys' here versus the issuer-key/kernel-key asymmetry of G4 and Section 4. Settle on one enumeration and use it everywhere.
- Section 1, contributions: the Lean bullet says the module 'collapses the runtime's six-gate conjunction' into three gates; Section 4 says four of the six are assumed rather than collapsed. Say 'abstracts away' not 'collapses', and drop 'falsifiable' for a definitional theorem.

- Section 3, worked envelope: rewrite as a genuine DSSE envelope with payloadType, base64 payload, and signatures, and show the decoded statement separately. Use the in-toto v1 statement type URI. Make predicateType a URI. State how lease {epoch, expiry} becomes leaseHash and how signerKids becomes signerKidsHash (is the array sorted? is order significant? if sorted, which kernel is 'local'?).
- Section 3, JCS paragraph: RFC 8785 does not perform Unicode normalization (no NFC/NFD); it defines escaping and sorts property names by UTF-16 code units. Two implementations fed differently-normalized but visually identical strings will produce different bytes. Correct the claim and say whether inputs are normalized upstream.
- Section 3, G4: state that signature array order is significant, or make G4 symmetric (one kid in each set, in either order) and say so. State whether IssuerKeys and KernelKeys must be disjoint. If a key appears in both lists, G3 (kid inequality) is the only independence check left.
- Section 3, G4 bundling: 'a signer outside both allowlists fails (G2) before (G4)' is false because predicateType is sender-controlled. Either give G4 its own code or explain what an attacker with the correct predicate string and an unpinned key receives (presumably a pre-gate signature failure, which Section 3 says is outside the taxonomy, in which case G4 is redundant with signature verification and should be dropped from the formal predicate).

### Questions for the authors

- Q1: Give the single authoritative list of binding-object fields, and the exact canonical preimage of leaseHash and signerKidsHash. Is signerKids order-significant? Is treatyId inside the subject digest, and if not, why not?
- Q2: Is signature slot order significant for G4 (issuer in slot 1, kernel in slot 2)? What happens when a key is present in both IssuerKeys and KernelKeys?
- Q3: In the shipped verifier, in what order are the two Ed25519 verifications, G1, and G2 evaluated? What exactly is returned to a sender who presents the correct predicateType string with an unpinned key?
- Q4: Where in the accept predicate is the comparison between the envelope's treatyScopeHash/ladderInterHash and the treaty resolved from the trust store? If it lives only in the operational verifier, what is the formal accept relation the paper is claiming?
- Q5: What is the full set of error codes emitted by verify_chio_bilateral_dsse_envelope plus the operational verifier in bilateral_verifier.rs? Are the five codes in the paper a projection of a larger enumeration?
- Q6: Is the revocation epoch global per verifier? What happens to every outstanding lease from every counterparty when one capability is revoked?
- Q7: What durability does continuation reserve/consume/release have across a verifier crash or across replicas? Can two replicas admit the same continuation?
- Q8: Can you provide a per-rejection-code negative matrix (fixture id, expected code, observed code, dispatch counter) run against the shipped verifier, and a microbenchmark of the verifier alone?
- Q9: How does a relying party verify a BBS-disclosed subset against the Ed25519-authoritative record without obtaining the full record?
- Q10: With strict predicate-type equality and no version tag, how do two organizations roll from v1 to v2 without a coordinated flag day?

### The one change that would most move the score

Replace the Section 3 accept predicate with the one the shipped verifier actually evaluates (including the trust-store comparisons of resolved treaty scope and ladder hashes, peer pins, and continuation state that the sibling-treaty and replay arguments depend on), make the binding-object and envelope specification consistent across the abstract, figure, prose, and worked example, and back it with a per-rejection-code negative matrix and a verifier-only microbenchmark reproducible from a downloadable artifact. That single change would convert the paper from a description of an intended format into a specification of shipped behavior, and would move me to a 4.
