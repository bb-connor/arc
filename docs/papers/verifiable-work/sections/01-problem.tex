\section{Work between organizations}
A buyer commissions a software review for 100 units. Its provider buys a
specialist's analysis for 60. The specialist delivers acceptable work, but the
provider crashes before completing the review. Two facts should survive: the
buyer has not received its result, and the specialist has earned payment.
Returning the buyer's funds must not erase the specialist's claim. Keeping the
specialist paid must not require access to the provider's private scheduler,
database or signing keys.

This example separates an agent workflow from a work economy. Within one
application, a coordinator can schedule tasks, allocate a budget and interpret
success. Between organizations, those decisions belong to different principals.
A remote operator can fork its database, sign contradictory promises or stop
responding. Its signature authenticates a statement; it cannot establish that
all its promises are jointly funded. Likewise, a payment receipt proves a
financial event, not that the agreed work was performed.

The unit of cooperation we propose is a \emph{work contract}: immutable terms
that identify a bounded task, its acceptance procedure and a funded obligation.
The receiver admits the task under its own authority. Evidence of execution and
acceptance can travel; the power to use the receiver's resources cannot be
installed by the arriving evidence. Payment becomes collectible through the
agreed settlement mechanism rather than a new discretionary promise from the
buyer.

This construction uses existing cryptography and settlement. It does not create
scarcity from signatures or equate useful work with consensus proof of work.
Bitcoin resolves competing spends through a shared history under explicit
assumptions~\cite{bitcoin}. Here the settlement domain supplies that history;
the harder application question is what evidence should make a particular work
obligation payable, and how that event relates to local execution.

Our result is a concrete composition with a narrow demonstration. We identify
the commitments each boundary must preserve, state the safety argument, and
make its failure cases executable. We also implement the obvious alternative:
an ordinary database escrow with the same transition rules. Its matching result
is part of the evaluation. The protocol's broader promise is that parties can
implement one public contract and change partners without adopting a common
application. That promise requires an independent interoperability and
integration study; it does not follow from the safety argument.
