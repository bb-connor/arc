\section{Work commitments}
\label{sec:contract}
A work commitment is constructed from decisions made by different authorities.
The owner allocates an allowance, a provider offers to perform a task, the
holder selects that offer, and the allocator freezes the choice. For paid work,
the payer then reserves funds under an agreement referring to the selected
call. Figure~\ref{fig:work} shows how execution and settlement use these records.

\subsection{Allocate an allowance}
A work slot specifies a task that may be delegated or assigned to a provider:
\[
 S=(i,h,E,A,b,c,t,d,Q).
\]
Here $i$ is the slot identifier, $h$ its holder, $E$ the permitted tool operations,
$A$ the permitted readers, $b$ the spending ceiling in currency $c$, $t$ the
expiry, $d$ the remaining delegation depth and $Q$ the acceptance predicate.
Root creation is an owner-controlled operation.

The holder signs a subdivision that names the parent allocation and the complete
child slot. The allocator accepts it only if the child's operations and readers
are subsets of the parent's, its expiry is no later, its depth is smaller and
its currency is unchanged. Child ceilings must satisfy
\[
 \sum_{j\in\operatorname{children}(S)} b_j\leq b_S.
\]
The allocator records the child and updates the parent's assigned amount in one
transaction. A slot with children cannot execute, and a selected slot cannot
acquire children. Thus the parent and its descendants cannot both spend the
same allowance. An internal slot's unused remainder can support later children.

Each allocation is identified by a digest of the allocator's persistent
namespace, the root slot and the target slot. This prevents an offer made for
one owner's allocation from being reused at another honest allocator that chose
the same local names. The child may have its own acceptance predicate: a survey
and a specialist review require different checks. The parent task still needs
its own result and acceptance.

\subsection{Select and seal an invocation}
The provider signs an offer containing the allocation and slot digests, its
receiving key, the tool operation and input, a price ceiling and an expiry.
The holder selects the offer together with a request identifier and the digest
of a capability issued by the receiver. Until the selection is sealed, a revision
check permits replacement while rejecting stale choices.

Sealing is the point after which the choice cannot change. The allocator first
freezes the selection, then stores a signed dispatch permit before returning
it. The permit contains the slot and signed selection. A receiver can check
these records without contacting the allocator at dispatch time; exclusive
allocation still depends on the allocator's protected state.

The receiver compares the permit with the caller, receiving key, capability,
tool, input and request actually presented. Its capability permits one invocation
of that operation with monetary ceilings no greater than the signed offer.
Validity, revocation and local policy are checked at admission. Consequently,
an offer for one input cannot authorize a different input merely because both
calls use the same tool.

\subsection{Reserve payment}
The payment agreement identifies the complete final request, payer, beneficiary,
verifier, asset, amount, settlement system and deadlines. Before dispatch, the
receiver requires evidence, under its chosen settlement observation rule, that
the agreement's allocation holds the required funds. The escrow identifier
includes the chain and contract, so identical allocation names on another rail
do not identify the same reserve.

A subcontract has its own backing. The intermediary in our example funds the
specialist from money it already controls. The payment it expects from the buyer
remains locked under a different agreement. The intermediary therefore bears
the subcontract cost if its own task fails; the specialist's earned payment does
not depend on completion of that task.

\subsection{Check the references}
The receiver connects the commitment to the program by checking its signed task
graph. The graph identifies tasks and their dependencies, assigns ceilings from
the program's declared pool, and carries routes naming the receiving kernels.
Its authorization records identify the capabilities permitted for those tasks.

Suppose the specialist's permit and capability authorize receiver $R_1$, but a
correctly signed graph routes the same task to $R_2$. Both sets of signatures
can verify even though the records disagree. The receiver rejects this
combination because its own key, the offer's receiving key and the signed
route's destination must match. Authenticating each record separately would
miss that comparison.

The same principle applies to the other references. The slot, request and graph
task must have one task identity. The graph allocation must name the delegated
allocation digest and agree on amount and currency. The permit and graph
authorization records must name the same capability. These checks establish
which invocation the otherwise separate records authorize.

The payment agreement authenticates the complete request, including its
delegation, graph and treaty references. Its financial allocation has a separate
identifier linked through that agreement. The references are acyclic: the request
contains admission evidence; the agreement commits to the request; the escrow
commits to the agreement. Delegation and admission records use canonical JSON
and versioned domains~\cite{jcs}; settlement decisions use EIP-712~\cite{eip712}.

The receiver installs these checks from protected configuration before both
fresh admission and recovery. Omitting request context or removing local
configuration therefore cannot select a path that skips them. The exact signed
fields and encodings are given in Appendix~\ref{app:profiles}.
