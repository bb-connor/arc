\section{Parties, authority and assumptions}
A work edge connects a payer $B$ and a provider $P$. A selected verifier $V$
decides the agreed acceptance predicate, a custody service retains the relevant
bytes, and a settlement domain $R$ allocates and transfers funds. One
organization may supply several services, but each role and key is explicit.
Distinct keys alone do not establish independent administration. A provider on
one edge can be the payer on another.

An agent chooses requests and may be malicious. The honest organization's
kernel mediates its resource access using locally activated trust roots,
bounded capabilities and current policy. The remote operator may control its
agents, signing keys, storage, messages and task implementation. The network
may delay, duplicate, reorder or lose messages. Processes may fail between
any two durable steps. Cryptographic authenticity cannot make a dishonest
operator's account of its own execution true.

The initial profile, F1, makes five assumptions. First, the honest party's local
enforcement and protected state work as specified. Second, signatures are
unforgeable and the selected hashes resist collisions. Third, the settlement
domain enforces exclusive allocations, atomic transitions and the chosen
finality rule; its token transfers exactly the agreed units. Fourth, the pinned
verifier applies the agreed predicate to the bound inputs and makes an
eligible decision available within its window. Fifth, the parties can obtain
the evidence and submit transactions needed to exercise a claim. Safety and
liveness depend on different subsets of these assumptions.

The verifier is a trusted decision authority, even when its checker is publicly
reproducible. Other readers can detect a wrong decision by rerunning the check;
F1 does not make the settlement contract execute that check. A compromised
verifier can approve bad work or withhold a decision. A compromised settlement
domain can violate backing. These are central trust dependencies, not details
of transport security.

Three ledgers describe different facts. The receiver's authorization ledger
answers whether an operation was admitted. The evidence record answers what
was observed and checked. The settlement ledger answers which funds are
reserved, payable or transferred. No record substitutes for the other two.
In particular, a tool whose effect is unknown after a crash remains unknown
even if the parties later release a payment hold.

We bound only the obligations admitted under the contract. The bound excludes
unapproved off-protocol spending, opportunity cost, loss of disclosed data,
verifier fees and transaction fees unless the terms explicitly reserve them.
The reference experiment uses one fixed-price result and one subcontracting
level. The composition argument applies to a finite set of distinct funded
edges; it neither proves that a cyclic task graph finishes nor implements
unrestricted recursive procurement.
