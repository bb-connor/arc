\section{Principals, resources and assumptions}
The \emph{owner} configures an allocator and creates a root contract. A
\emph{holder} may subdivide its allocation and select an offer. A
\emph{receiver} offers a tool operation and issues the native capability under
which it will run. A courier transports signed records. An organization can
occupy several roles; distinct keys alone do not establish independent
administration. Holders and receivers in the dynamic profile are previously
qualified keys explicitly permitted by the owner. Discovery selects among them;
it does not enroll an unknown party or authorize a new disclosure.

Agents and couriers may be malicious. Messages may be delayed, duplicated,
reordered or lost. Processes may crash between durable steps. The honest
owner's allocator serializes allocation and preserves its state. Each receiving
key has one protected native custody and budget domain. Cloning those keys and
databases into independently spendable forks violates the assumptions.
Signatures authenticate statements; they do not prevent a trusted issuer that
has been compromised from making contradictory statements.

We distinguish three experimental profiles. \textbf{D1} implements dynamic
allocation and locally checked dispatch permits. It assumes honest allocator
enforcement, collision-resistant hashes, unforgeable signatures, accurate
validity checks, and the receiver's capability, durable admission and payment
enforcement. Its effect and reader sets are declarations whose completeness
depends on the qualified host and tool confinement. Set inclusion alone does
not prove information-flow security or prevent an arbitrary tool from using an
undeclared channel. The local implementation uses a durable integer payment
fixture; its budget allocations are ceilings, not proof of deposited assets.

\textbf{S1} implements additive evolution of Chio's existing swarm authority.
It uses signed task graphs, attenuation witnesses, routes, revocation epochs,
allocations and single-use continuations. Each graph has one protected current
head, and one protected authority controls the declared pool across that graph's
versions. The installer serializes successors; receivers resolve the exact
version named by a request and preserve native continuation custody. Reusing a
pool in independently provisioned graphs or stores is outside this allocation
discipline. The evaluated graph growth is additive: it preserves permissions,
expiry, declared capacity and prior artifacts instead of reclaiming or
transferring them. Ordinary capability and treaty checks apply at dispatch.

\textbf{F1} implements funded, fixed-price work claims. A payer $B$ reserves
funds for provider $P$ in settlement domain $R$. A selected verifier $V$ applies
the agreed predicate to bound inputs and outputs. F1 additionally assumes that
the rail enforces exclusive allocations and atomic transfers, the asset
transfers the agreed units, and the finality observation is sound. Collection
requires available evidence, eligible verifier decisions and transaction
inclusion. The verifier remains a trusted decision authority even if others
can rerun its checker. D1, S1 and F1 share architectural boundaries but have
separate evaluated trajectories. No reported execution combines all three
into one dynamic, funded, independently operated swarm.

Three kinds of state answer different questions. Allocation state records
which capacity is committed. Native custody records what was admitted and
observed during execution. Settlement state records what is reserved, payable
and transferred. An unknown tool effect remains unknown after a financial
refund. A sealed allocation remains committed after a network timeout. A
historical receipt cannot authorize a new invocation.

The bounds concern operations admitted under these profiles. They exclude
off-protocol spending, opportunity cost, loss of disclosed data and fees unless
those costs are explicitly reserved. D1 supports finite trees with bounded
wire objects; S1 grows finite signed graphs; F1 evaluates distinct funded edges.
These profiles do not prove that
an arbitrary task decomposes correctly, that every participant remains
available or that the work is economically useful.
